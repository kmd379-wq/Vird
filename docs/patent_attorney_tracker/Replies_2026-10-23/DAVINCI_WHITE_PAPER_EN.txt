II. Unique Strategic Reasons to Consider Filing First in the United States 
The United States offers distinct strategic advantages as a first-filing jurisdiction.  
A first filing in the United States can confer strategic benefits unavailable elsewhere—
particularly for innovators who wish to protect both trade se crets and patent rights while 
deferring large, deadline-driven costs. 
A. The U.S. One-Year Grace Period 
Because U.S. patent law affords a one-year grace period 10 for disclosures made by 
the inventor, filing in the United States may be the only remaining option if self-disclosure 
has already compromised the ability to patent in Europe, where grace periods are far more 
restrictive. 11  Thus, even when an EPO filing is no longer viable, a U. S. filing may still 
remain possible. 
Moreover, U.S. law provides no grace peri od for secret commercial activity that 
predates the earliest priority date—inclu ding offers for sale made under NDAs. As 
confirmed in Helsinn Healthcare S.A. v. Teva Pharmaceutical USA, Inc., confidential sales 
may trigger the on-sale bar. 
B. Filing First in the United States to Pr eserve Trade Secret Rights Through Non-
Publication 
United States law uniquely allows an applicant to prevent publication of a U.S. 
patent application when two conditions are satisfied: (1) a non-publication request is filed 
at the time of the U.S. filing, and (2) the applicant certifies that the invention “has not been 
and will not be the subject of an applicati on filed in another country” that requires 18-
month publication. 12 

[PAGE 6]
 
6 
 
If these conditions are met, the USPTO must hold the application in confidence and 
will not publish it at 18 months. This enab les the applicant to maintain trade secret 
protection indefinitely while also preserving the right to obtain a U.S. patent. This strategic, 
non-published U.S. regular application—w hat you call a “new” U.S. Submarine 
Application (as opposed to the old Lemelson-style Submarines – see Section XIV below)—
creates a deferral mechanism unavailable in Europe, China or the rest of the world. 
By contrast, if the innovator files first in any jurisdiction with mandatory 18-month 
publication (e.g., the EPO, France, the U.K., Italy, China, or WIPO), the applicant 
immediately and permanently loses the ab ility to pursue a valid U.S. Submarine 
application, even if the fore ign application is withdrawn before publication. Because the 
eventual publication destroys trade secrecy, this forecloses a powerful cost-management 
and secrecy-preservation strategy. 
 
C. Europe and China Do Not Provide an Equivalent Non-Publication Option 
Neither the European Patent Conventi on nor the China National Intellectual 
Property Administration offers any mechanism comparable to 37 C.F.R. § 1.213. In both 
systems, applications are publ ished at 18 months as a matte r of course, meaning a first 
filing in those jurisdictions necessarily tr iggers publication, thereby extinguishing the 
ability to maintain trade secret rights past that point. 
 
D. Strategic Implications for Startups, Knowhow-rich Corporations, and Resource-
Constrained Innovators 
For startups, who in today’s uncertain world are best advised to “hope for the best 
and plan for the worst”, a first filing in the United States is almost always the prudent 
choice when the technology is amenable to trade secret protection. Filing first in the U.S. 
preserves trade secret status, reserves patent rights, allows deferral of foreign filings and 
their associated costs, and positions the comp any to make better-informed decisions once 
funding, market validation, and competitive intelligence mature. 
 
E. Preserving All Filing Options While Controlling Costs 
Because foreign filings and translati ons generate “deadline-driven cost 
explosions,” filing first in the United States gives innovators a critical buffer against budget 
volatility. For technologies su ch as manufacturing processes or SaaS systems—both of 
which may be kept confidential indefinitely—avoiding a mandatory 18-month publication 
prevents the creation of “monster” prior art (the publication of one’s own patent 
application) that would otherwise obliterate trade secret rights.

Thus, U.S. first filing is not merely a legal tactic; it is a financial risk-management 
strategy that maximizes long-term optionality. 
 
F. Even in Secrecy, You Can Still Prove Your Rights to Stakeholders 
A secret U.S. application does not l eave the innovator empty-handed in due 
diligence or investment negotiations. Applicants retain an official U.S. filing receipt, and 
the ability to order a sealed, certified copy of the application as filed and, eventually, a 
notice of allowance.  These documents are fu lly adequate to demonstrate priority and 
ownership to investors, acquirers, or partners—provided disclosures occur under NDA to 
avoid destroying trade secret st atus. Notably, U.S. law does not  require that an applicant 
keep the invention secret to maintain a non-publication request; secrecy is only necessary 
to preserve trade secret rights, not to maintain the application’s unpublished status. 13 
III. Framing the Landscape of IP Protection Strategies 
In order to appreciate the strategic advantages available under U.S. law, it is useful 
to lay out the traditional intellectual-property protection paths and contrast them with the 
new hybrid strategies uniquely available in the United States.  Historically, innovators have 
understood only three principal strategies for protecting their technology: 
1. Filing for patent protecti on, thereby pursuing exclusiv e rights but necessarily 
triggering publication at 18 months, which destroys trade secret status; 
2. Maintaining trade secret protection, thereby avoiding publication and cost 
escalations but forfeiting the ability to obtain patent rights, particularly where 
commercial embodiments reveal the underlying technology; and 
3. Employing a “mini-Sub” strategy, na mely, successive refiling of U.S. 
provisional applications to preserve trade secret status, claim “patent pending” 
for marketing purposes, and postpone cost-intensive deadlines—while retaining 
the ability to convert to a regular U.S. or international application within the 
allowable priority window. 
These three strategies, however, represent only the starting point. Under U.S. law, 
there exist three additional hybr id mechanisms, unavailable el sewhere, that dramatically 
expand an innovator’s ability to sequence patent filings, defer costs, and preserve secrecy.  
A. The U.S. Submarine Patent Strategy 
A regular, non-published U.S. application—filed with a non-publication request—
may remain secret and pending for years until  the applicant elects to permit issuance. 
Because the application avoids the mandatory publication regimes of Europe, China, and 
WIPO, it allows the innovator to maintain tr ade secret protection while preserving U.S. 

[PAGE 8]
 
8 
 
patent rights of early priority. This strategy enables the applicant to delay cost explosions, 
litigation risks, and irreversible disclosure events until commercially optimal. 
 
B. The “UFO” Design Filing Strategy (Unidentifiable Filed Ornamental Design) 
If the initial U.S. utility application contains drawings that show a complete set of 
views of at least one orname ntal design of the invention (including physical products or 
GUIs), the applicant may file U.S. divisional  design applications at any time while the 
utility case remains pending. Because U.S. design patent term is measured from grant 
rather than filing, this tactic can yield design protection extending well beyond the typical 
20-year limit associated with ut ility patents. The result is a strategically timed, surprise 
form of design protection that is virtually unknown outside the United States. 
C. A Combined Submarine + UFO Design Strategy 
By integrating design figures within a non-published utility application, the 
innovator can simultaneously (1) preserve secrecy through the Submarine application and 
(2) later file divisional designs that issue into long-lived, enforceable design patents. This 
hybrid method leverages the secrecy advant ages of the Submar ine strategy and the 
extended-term advantages of late-filed design divisionals. 
Consequently, where traditional doctrine  taught that innova tors must choose 
between trade secret protection and patent protection—each mutually exclusive of the 
other—the availability of th e Submarine, UFO, and combin ed strategies fundamentally 
alters that calculus. Only U.S. law permits these hybrid approaches, allowing innovators to 
(1) preserve secrecy far longer than any fore ign system permits, (2) reserve patent rights 
without triggering premature pub lication, (3) strategically t ime the entry into foreign 
systems when resources and commercial circumstances justify, and (4) extend enforceable 
design protection beyond the conventional 20-year framework.  
In short, U.S. law offers a unique tool kit unavailable elsewhere, transforming the 
landscape of IP decision-making and enabli ng innovators—particularly startups and 
knowhow-rich organizations—to na vigate risk, cost, and disc losure with far greater 
precision.

This traditional path unfolds through the following sequence: 
 
A. Applicant’s Publications are the Worst Prior Art:  
Once an inventor makes a public disclosure —whether deliberate or inadvertent—the 
publication becomes prior art against the inventor’s later filings. Under U.S. law, applicant-
originated disclosures may still receive limited grace-period treat ment, but elsewhere 
(notably Europe) such disclo sures often terminate patentability entirely. This creates 
immediate urgency to file promptly and globally before any publication. 
 
B. The Paris Convention’s 12-Month Deadline 
Within twelve months of th e first filing, the applicant must file al l corresponding 
regular national applications (for example, in Taiwan, which is not a PCT member) or, if 
publication has already taken pl ace, irrevocably lose his ri ght to claim priority. This 
includes the substantial cost of  preparing formal patent sp ecifications, drawings, claims, 
and translations as required. 
 
C. If a PCT Application Is Filed: The 30- or 31-Month National/Regional Phase Deadline

10 
 
The Patent Cooperation Treaty gives applicants a temporary reprieve, allowing deferral 
until roughly month 30 or 31. At that point, the applicant must again incur substantial filing, 
translation, and local-counsel expenses or permanently abandon rights in each non-entered 
territory. 
 
D. Mandatory 18-Month Publication 
Every PCT or foreign application publishes at 18 months from the earliest priority date, 
and failure to file in a country before public ation destroys rights in  that country because 
the applicant’s own published application be comes prior art against itself.  Thus, 
publication operates as a compul sory trigger: pay the high international costs now, or 
irrevocably forfeit protection in each country in which you have not effectively filed. 
E. Renewal Fees Begin Before Grant in Many Jurisdictions 
In Europe and many other systems, annua l renewal fees begin early—even during 
pendency. The United States is one of the few exceptions, requiring maintenance fees only 
after grant, at only three points in time.  For applicants managing multiple national filings, 
these annual annuities rapidly accumulate into substantial financial burdens. 
F. Examination, Rejection, Appeal, and Acceptance Timelines 
Applicants typically wait years for examin ation results. For ex ample, national-phase 
prosecution in Europe or the United States ma y take three to five years or longer before 
final allowance or rejection.  Throughout this period, the application continues to generate 
expenditures for attorney responses, amendmen ts, translations, annuities, and strategic 
decision-making. 
G. The Result: A Pattern of Predictable “Cost Explosions” 
Foreign filings multiply expenses: multiple  patent attorneys, multiple languages, 
multiple official fees, multiple translati on costs, and multiple ma intenance obligations. 
None of these costs can be avoided once a pub lication occurs. Each is driven by statutory 
deadlines—not business readin ess, not market timing, not budget cycles.  Hence, after 
publication, the applicant must either (1) pay the “ransom”—the cumulative global filing, 
translation, and prosecution expe nses required to preserve pa tent rights, or (2) renounce 
rights in every jurisdiction not entered before publication, knowing that the applicant’s own 
publication will enable competitors to freely ma nufacture, use, or sell the invention in  
countries in which they have not reserved their patent rights. 
This dilemma is most acute because the en trepreneur rarely knows—at the 12- or 18-
month point—whether the invention is commercially viable, or whether the cost of global 
patenting is justified. Nevert heless, the system plays upon the entrepreneur’s natural

V. The Typical Alternative: Pure Trade Secret Protection 
A trade secret–based IP strategy offers the inverse risk profile of the traditional patent 
strategy. It avoids global pate nt deadlines and the cascading costs associated with them, 
and it can—at least in theory—last indefinitely.  
Due to the cost burdens of the traditional patent filing strategy, many innovators 
consider avoiding patent filings altogether. A trade secret strategy avoids the 12-month 
Paris deadline, avoids the 18-month publication cliff, avoids or postpones translation and 
annuity costs, and avoids the escalating, deadline-driven “ransom” of foreign filing fees.

accidentally—protection terminates permanently  unless a patent filing had been made 
before such publication (or with in the U.S. grace period, wh ere applicable).  Innovators 
therefore face a binary historical choice: file  patents and lose trade secrecy, or maintain

A. The Trade Secret Path 
Under a trade secret–only strategy, th e innovator creates technology capable of 
being kept confidential—freque ntly a manufacturing process or a SaaS architecture 
operating behind a secure firewall.  He must  encapsulate the trade secret, typically by 
preparing a written description (o ften in the format of a pate nt application) and storing it 
securely, limiting access strictly to those who sign NDAs and have a need to know. 
He can relies on prior-user rights, but only in the jurisdiction where the invention is actually 
practiced—rights that generally protect against infringement suits but do not prevent others 
from independently patenting abroad.  He maintains trade secret protection only so long as 
the information is not generally known, the information retains commercial value because 
it is secret, it is identifiable as such, and the owner has taken reasonable steps to preserve 
secrecy. 
In this way, trade secret protection is theoretically perpetual. But once the 
information becomes public—even inadvert ently—protection terminates and cannot be 
revived. 
 
B. The Critical Weakness: Publication of the Trade Secret 
If the secret is ever disc losed—through product sales, disclosures to customers, 
careless handling, or even inte rnal error—trade secret prot ection evaporates immediately 
leaving the innovator with nothing unless a patent  application was filed before disclosure 
(or, in the United States, within the one-year  grace period when applicable). Thus, the 
entrepreneur risks losing all rights if  commercial activity exposes the underlying 
technology before a protective filing is made. 
 
C. The Core Tension: Patent vs. Trade Secret as Mutually Exclusive Choices 
The conventional wisdom has long held th at (1) filing a stra tegically important 
patent application inevitably results in public ation at 18 months, and publication destroys 
trade secrecy; and (2) preserving trade s ecrets precludes obtaini ng patent protection—
particularly when the act of selling a pr oduct discloses the i nventive technology.  
Consequently, innovators historically have been forced to choose between patents or 
secrecy, but not both. 
 
D. Why Documentation Matters 
Even within a trade secret–only strate gy, proper documentation is critical.  
Preparing a patent-style disclosure serves as  a formal record identifying what the trade 

[PAGE 13]
 
13 
 
secret consists of—an essential requirement for successful litigation in the event of trade 
secret theft.  But once the product of a secret process is offered for sale, even under NDA, 
the innovator may lose the ability to later seek U.S. patent protection unless a filing 
predates that offer. After publication of the technology, the U.S. one-year grace period 
begins to run—but publication simultaneously destroys trade secrecy. 
 
E. Logical Outcomes Under the Traditional Framework 
Under the traditional dichotomy: 
Filing patents forfeits trade secret prot ection (because of 18-month publication). 
Preserving trade secrets forfeits patent prot ection (because later sales or disclosures 
extinguish novelty).  Thus, until the synthesis of the U.S.-only hybrid strategies discussed 
herein, innovators faced only two choices:  File patents, or preserve trade secrets. 
 
F. The Truth Available Under U.S. Law 
Contrary to the historical assumption, trade secret rights and U.S. patent rights are 
not mutually exclusive.  A properly manage d, non-published U.S. Submarine application 
can preserve patent rights and enable the innovator to maintain trade secret protection well 
beyond 18 months, defeating the forced-choice paradigm. 
The remainder of this pres entation introduces the U. S.-only hybrid strategies—
Submarine, Mini-Sub, and UFO design fi lings—that defeat this dilemma by: 
postponing deadlines, maintaining secr ecy until strategical ly advantageous, 
preventing cost explosions, and enabling innova tors to switch paths only when evidence, 
markets, and funding justify it. 
 
VI. The Successive Refiling (“Mini-Sub”) Strategy 
The “mini-Sub” strategy—built around the successive refiling of U.S. provisional 
patent applications—offers innovators a low- cost mechanism to preserve trade secrecy, 
maintain “patent pending” status, and pos tpone major cost commitments, all while 
retaining the flexibility to seek full pate nt protection when timing and resources are 
optimal. 
 
A. Core Mechanics of the Mini-Sub Strategy 

[PAGE 14]
 
14 
 
A U.S. provisional applicati on, colloquially termed a “m ini-Sub,” is filed, then 
optionally updated and successively refiled near the end of each Paris-year cycle. Because 
provisional applications never publish, this strategy allows applicants to maintain secrecy 
while preserving forward-looking patent opportunities. 
Each mini-Sub has the following defining ch aracteristics: (1) the preservation of 
Trade Secret protection and (2) the ability to  claim “patent pending”, (3) deferral of 
deadline-drive cost explosions , and (4) optional conversion to  formal applications.    
Because provisional applicati ons remain confidential and do  not trigger publication, the 
innovator maintains trade secret  protection indefinitely—so l ong as secrecy is otherwise 
maintained.  In addition, filing one or more mini-Subs enables the innovator to accurately 
market the technology as “patent pending,” even while maintainin g trade secrecy.   
The mini-Sub structure delays cost-intensive foreign filings , formal patent drafting, 
translations, and national-phase commitments until the innovator chooses to convert to a 
regular application.  Still furt her, if circumstances indica te that the technology may not 
remain secret much longer, or  if market validation warrants  more robust protection, the 
applicant can file a full U.S. or  international application and reach back in time up to just 
under 12 months by claiming priority to the most recent mini-Sub. 
 
B. Understanding the Paris-Deadline Cycle Under the Mini-Sub Framework

The mini-Sub strategy operates as a rolling one-year cycle.  Near the end of Year 
1, the innovator may either (a) file a regular application (U .S., non-U.S., or PCT), or (b)

refile an improved or  updated provisional—thus resetting the one-y ear decision window. 
This process may continue through Year 2, Ye ar 3, and so on. Ultimately, a formal filing 
(U.S., PCT, or non-U.S. filing) may claim priority to a mini-Sub filed within the last year. 
Crucially, refiling restarts the up to 21-year patent term clock, preserving long-term value 
when the innovator eventually pursues full patent protection. 
 
C. The Limits and Risks of the Mini-Sub Strategy 
Despite its flexibility, the mini-Sub approach  carries important li mitations.  First, 
there is the Secret Offer-for-Sale Risk.  Unless a priority claim predates the first secret offer 
for sale, U.S. rights may be jeopardized if later challenged.  Thus , mini-Subs are most 
useful when U.S. rights matter and priority can be established before any offer for sale, or 
U.S. rights are less important than the inexpensive ability to claim patent pending or to 
claim priority in non-U.S. jurisdictions. 
A mini-Sub is not a substitute for a S ubmarine application. Because a mini-Sub 
cannot remain pending forever (it expires at 12 months), it does not provide the long-term 
secrecy or pendency advantages of a full U.S. Submarine ap plication, which can remain 
pending—and unpublished—for many years.  Howe ver, a mini-Sub can only reach back 
to support a claim of priority for one year.  Beyond that, earlier filings cannot be captured 
into the chain. 
 
D. Strategic Assessment: Helpful, but Not Ideal 
While the mini-Sub strategy provides meaningful benefits—including trade secret 
preservation, optionality, and cost deferral—it is not ideal for innovators who require long-
term secrecy, multi-year pendency, prot ection in the face of potential secret 
commercialization, or early priority.  Nevert heless, the mini-Sub re mains better than 
nothing, particularly for founders and early-stage companies navigating uncertain funding, 
unclear market validation, or evolving product designs. 
 
VII. The U.S. Submarine Application:  A Long-Term, Non-Published Patent 
Strategy Unique to the United States 
A U.S. Submarine application—a regular utility patent application filed with a non-
publication request—constitutes one of the most powerful secrecy-preserving mechanisms 
available under the law. Unlike provisional (mini-Sub) filings, a Submarine application 
may remain pending and unpublished for many year s, enabling the innovator to maintain 

[PAGE 16]
 
16 
 
trade secret protection while simultaneously preserving enforceable U.S. patent rights that 
can be activated at a strategically chosen moment. 
A. The Concept and Advantages of the U.S. Submarine Application 
 
Fig. 4: U.S. Submarine Framework 
A U.S. Submarine application stays fully  pending and secret until the applicant 
elects to permit the patent to issue. An applicant may even reach a stage where the USPTO 
has allowed claims, yet continue keeping the application pendi ng for further years—
maintaining trade secrecy, refining claim sc ope, and preserving opt ionality regarding 
timing of grant.  Because third parties ca nnot discover the existence of an unpublished 
Submarine application, a Submarine patent ap plication, once granted, often “surfaces” as 
a surprise, revealing patent rights that competitors had no way to anticipate. 
In addition, an applicant may also c hoose to publish the technology separately—
sacrificing trade secrecy intentionally—to prevent competitors from securing parallel 
patent rights in othe r jurisdictions. Notably, however, secr ecy is not required to maintain 
non-publication status for the Submarine; it is required only for preservation of trade secret 
rights.  In other words, disc losure of the technology does not trigger a requirement to 
inform the US patent office in the way that filing a non-U.S. patent application does. 
B. Procedural Requirements for Maintaining Submarine Status 
To obtain and preserve Submarine status , a non-publication request must be filed 
together with the U.S. regular application.  The applicant must not file, or must timely 
notify the USPTO if it has filed, any corresponding foreign or international application that

17 
 
would require 18-month publicatio n; failure to notify within the statutory time window 
results in automatic abandonment.  As long as the applicant complies with these conditions, 
the U.S. application remains unpublished indefinitely. 
The Submarine strategy ensures that the ap plicant need not relinquish trade secret 
rights until the precise scope of allowable claims is known. 
 
C. Strategic Benefits of Long Pendency 
A Submarine application allows the applicant to (1) delay cost explosions—
including translations, foreign filings, and large attorney expenditures—until business 
conditions justify the investment; (2) preserve the option to file continuation-in-part (CIP) 
applications, creating improved embodiments with their own later filing dates; (3) maintain 
trade secret protection fo r as long as the technology remains confidential; and 
(4) retain U.S. patent rights even if s ecrecy is later lost, whether through employee 
misconduct, competitor discovery, or unexpected disclosure. 
The Submarine is thus less fragile than a pure trade secret strategy because patent 
rights are preserved even in the ev ent of involuntary loss of secrecy. 
 
D. Interaction with International Patent Rights 
International protection remains available so  long as the invention remains secret. 
The applicant must simply notify the USPTO of  any foreign filing within the prescribed 
period so that the U.S. application can be published.  Importantly, European patent 
applications filed by competitors before th e Submarine’s earliest priority date, but 
published after that date, do not impair U. S. patentability, nor  do competitor U.S. 
applications filed after the Submarine’s priority date. Thus, a Submarine applicant may still 
exclude earlier European filers from the U.S. market. 
E. Strategic Options Based on Priority Structures 
The Submarine strategy enables multiple sophisticated maneuvers.  One is known 
as the Second Paris Tactic.  If the applicant learns that the technology has entered the public 
domain only within the time window of 12 mont hs to 16 months of the filing the U.S. 
provisional to which the U.S. Submarine ma y claim priority, the applicant may forgo 
claiming priority in the Submarine application (essentially by waiting to the last minute to 
claim it and then deciding not to claim it) and use the Submarine application as the priority 
basis for an international application—assuming no on-sale bar applies. 
Another maneuver: the applicant can elect a dual-track strategy.  If secrecy remains 
intact, the applicant may file internationally without claiming  priority to the Submarine,

while retaining the Submarine (converted into a non-Sub due to the non-US filing) 
exclusively for U.S. purposes. A recently file d provisional (a mini- Sub filing) may serve 
as the priority anchor for the international case only if the Submarine is abandoned before 
any publication. 
The applicant may even redact portions of the Submarine application and keep them 
secret while submitting only a non-redacted portion for publication by the USPTO and for 
international filing—thus “hav ing one’s cake and eating it t oo.” The redacted material 
remains entirely secret; the published material supports foreign rights. 14 
VIII. Managing a U.S. Sub marine Application: Requirements, Timing, and 
Strategic Execution 
The effective use of a U.S. Submar ine application demands disciplined 
management. Because the Submarine strategy hinges on secrecy, pendency, and informed 
timing, the applicant mu st actively supervise filings—not only in the United States but 
across all jurisdictions where disclosure, commer cialization, or patent activity may affect 
trade secret rights or the viability of the Submarine itself. 
A. Foundational Requirements for a Properly Maintained Submarine Application 
A Submarine application must be (1) pr operly filed with a valid non-publication 
request, ensuring that the application remains confidential unless and until the applicant 
elects otherwise; (2) monitored in relation to the applicant’s own foreign filings, meaning 
that the applicant must ensure no foreign or international applications are filed—or, if they 
are, they must timely notify the USPTO so as to avoid automatic abandonment; (3) aligned 
with commercial conduct, including vigilance concerning any activity that may constitute 
an “offer for sale,” whether domestic or fore ign, public or confid ential; and (4) managed 
alongside competitor behavior, as third-party filings, publications, or product releases may 
influence the strategic timing of continuations, divisionals, or foreign filings. 
 
B. Maintaining Trade Secret Protection During Pendency 
To preserve trade secret protection wh ile the Submarine re mains pending, the 
applicant must keep the tec hnology genuinely c onfidential. The Submarine application 
itself remains secret by operation of U.S. law,  but trade secret stat us depends on actual 
secrecy practices—NDA use, restricted access, compartmentalization, and careful internal 
controls. 15 
 
C. Preparing the Submarine for Dual Use: Domestic and International

should function as a potential priority documen t for filings outside the United States—to 
which priority may be cla imed within 12 months of filing under a “Second Paris” 
strategy—to preserve rights abroad if and wh en the applicant determines that foreign 
protection is warranted. 
 
D. Long-Term Pendency: Keeping the Submarine Submerged 
A U.S. patent application can remain pending for up to twenty years from its filing 
date, provided the applicant files continuations , divisional applica tions, continuation-in-
part applications, and requests for continued examination (RCEs). 
This procedural arch itecture—unique in its  flexibility—permits an innovator to (1) 
continuously adjust claim scope as the mark et evolves, (2) pres erve secrecy until 
commercial conditions justify surfacing, and (3) shield the technology from publication in 
ways impossible under European, Chinese, or other foreign regimes. 
Importantly, U.S. law permits amendmen ts, intermediate generalizations, and 
“cherry-picking” in ways that foreign jurisd ictions significantly restrict, making it easier 
to build derivative applications that keep the Submarine pending. 
E. The Strategic Significance of Avoiding Publication 
If publication of the Submarine applicatio n is avoided, then trade secret rights 
remain intact for as long as the underlying technology stays confid ential. The applicant 
retains the ability to file va lid foreign applications year s later, provided no publication—
by the applicant or others—has occurred in the interim.  The applicant avoids deadline-
driven cost explosions, preserving forei gn rights only when and where justified by 
commercial evidence.  Thus, the Submarine strategy creates a near-unique environment in 
which the innovator can delay irreversible de cisions regarding disc losure, foreign filing, 
and cost commitments until th e market, funding landscape, and competitive intelligence 
warrant them. 
F. Summary: When Submarines Are Most Useful 
U.S. Submarine applications are particularly advantageous for technologies capable 
of being kept secret (e.g., manufacturing pr ocesses, SaaS operations, black-box systems), 
where the U.S. market is critical, when immedi ate foreign protection is not essential, and 
where the innovator seeks to control timing, preserve secrecy, and avoid or postpone global 
cost escalation. At the same time, the appli cant must recognize that  foreign competitors 
could independently file patent applications and obtain patent rights on similar technology

defense may prevent infringement liability, but it does not prevent othe rs from patenting.

may be to file the contents  of the Submarine internati onally or publish the technology 
intentionally, thereby creating prior art that  prevents competitor s from obtaining valid 
patents elsewhere. 
IX. Divisional Design Patent: the “UFO ” (“Unidentified Filed Ornamental”) 
Design 
A further U.S.-only tactic complements the Submarine approach: filing design 
patent applications—what we call “UFO” de signs—as divisionals or  continuations off a 
pending U.S. utility application that already integrates complete design drawings.  Properly 
executed, this tactic ultimately yields surprise-effect design rights that can issue quickly 
and, crucially, enjoy a fixed post-grant term measured from grant, not from the original 
utility filing date while benefiting from the original utility application effective filing date 
to define the prior art. The result is a strategically timed form of protection that can extend 
beyond the practical 20-year arc of the utility family. 
 
A. What the UFO Design Strategy Is 
If a first filing (which need not be the filed in the U.S. first) contains drawings that 
show a full set of views for at least one ornamental design of  the invention—covering 
physical articles and graphical user interfaces (GUIs)—the applicant may, any time during 
pendency of the related, later-filed U.S. utility patent application, file a design application 
as a divisional/continuation to  seek protection for those de corative/ornamental aspects. 
Because most non-U.S. practitioners do not look  for or recognize these integrated design 
disclosures in a pending U.S. case as inchoate design rights, the ultimate grant of such a 
design can come as a surprise even to seasone d non-U.S. counsel. In this sense, they are 
“Unidentifiable Filed Ornamentals”: the design rights are “filed” but hard to identify until 
they surface as issued design patents. No other country or  region offers this precise 
combination of timing, pendency, and grant-based term.

combination yields a portfolio architecture that  is exceedingly difficult for competitors to

A U.S. utility application, filed with a non-publication request, begins the process.

The hybrid strategy’s force lies in its time asymmetr y: competitors proceed 
assuming that what they see is all that exists, unaware that a confidential Submarine 
application may still be pending and that mu ltiple waves of design patents may surface 
later—each with long grant-based term s and immediate enforcement capacity. 
This architecture gives innovators extended leverage well beyond normal patent horizons, 
layered enforcement options (utility, design, tr ade secret), cost de ferral, sometimes for 
many years, and surprise enforcement at commercially advantageous moments.

XIII. Practical Drafting and Contracting Considerations for NDAs and Joint-
Development Agreements in Light of the Submarine Strategy 
The effective implementation of a U.S. Submarine strategy depends not only on 
filing mechanics but also on the careful structuring of non-disclosure agreements (NDAs) 
and joint-development agreements (JDAs). B ecause Submarine applications derive much 
of their strategic power from secrecy, timing, and the avoidance of triggering events such 
as foreign filings or inadvert ent offers for sale, parties co llaborating on technology must 
contract with an eye toward preserving these options. 
 
A. The Critical Role of Monitoring 
Successful deployment of a Submarine st rategy requires ongoing monitoring of 
both the innovator’s own actions and those of potential collaborators or competitors. This 
includes tracking disclosures, testing activities, offers for sale, and any filing behavior that 
might compromise secrecy or priority. Without active mon itoring, the advantages of a 
Submarine application may be unintentionally forfeited. 
B. Recommended NDA and JDA Provisions 
Where collaboration is contemplated, agreements should incorporate clauses 
specifically designed to safeguard Submarine-related rights. Such provisions may include: 
(1) Prohibition on Unauthorized Submar ine Filings: The party receiving the 
confidential information shoul d be forbidden from filing any Submarine application 
without explicit prior permission from the di sclosing party with a further safeguard being 

[PAGE 29]
 
29 
 
that, if no permission is sought within a defined time window, ownership of any such 
Submarine filing by the receiving party automatically reverts to the disclosing party;  
(2) Non-Challenge Clauses: the receiving party should ag ree not to challenge the 
disclosing party’s patent rights on the basis of  any alleged offer for sale, which seeks to 
protect against opportunistic i nvocation of an on-sale bar in circumstances where the 
parties intended confidentiality; 
(3) Strict Confidentiality  Treatment for Any Argua ble Offer for Sale: Any 
solicitation, quotation, or proposal that could conceivably be characterized as an “offer for 
sale” should be deemed strictly confidential and treated as such by both parties; 
(4) Non-Characterization Clauses: th e parties may stipulate that any 
communication directed to the r eceiving party shall not be construed as an offer for sale, 
ensuring that cooperative technical exchanges do not later become grounds for invalidity 
arguments; 
(5) Liquidated Damages for Breach: if conf idential information relating to any 
arguable offer for sale is wrongfully disclosed or later used to invalidate the disclosing 
party’s patent rights, the agreement may provide that liquidated damages become payable. 
 
C. Practical Consequences 
These contractual protections ensure that  the Submarine strategy remains viable 
even in multi-party developm ent environments. They prevent inadvertent triggering of 
statutory bars, guard against strategic game smanship between collaborating companies, 
and ensure that neither party’s conduct compromises long-term secrecy or patentability. 
Parallel to these contractual considerations , applicants should recall that utility 
applications may be kept pending for up to twenty years through the filing of continuations 
(CONs), divisionals (DIVs), continuations-i n-part (CIPs), and requests for continued 
examination (RCEs). This long-term pendenc y reinforces the S ubmarine strategy by 
allowing claims to evolve over time, while secrecy is preserved. 
Similarly, in the hybrid Submarine-plus-UFO framework, UFO design filings may 
be prosecuted as divisional design applicati ons, with the surprising advantage that these 
design patents grant for a fift een years from the date of gr ant, making late-filed design 
divisionals potent long-term enforcement tools.

rights at allowance do not trigger a separate limitations bar, and patentees frame damages 
by reference to the statute rather than the moment of allowance. 
 
A. A Supreme Court Clarification on “Laches” in Patent Damages 
The U.S. Supreme Court has made clear th at laches cannot ba r a legal claim for 
damages brought within the Patent Act’s si x-year look-back pe riod. In SCA Hygiene 
Products Aktiebolag v. First Qu ality Baby Products, LLC, the Court held that applying 
laches within a statutory limitations period would improperly override Congress’s 
judgment and the traditional role of equitable defenses. 20. Thus, when a patentee sues for 
damages within the § 286 window, laches cannot be interposed to cut off otherwise timely 
damages. 21 
B. Using Sub and UFO Tactics Is Lawful, Defensive, and Widely Available 
Employing Submarine and UFO design tactics is simply taking advantage of U.S. 
law—an option open to everyone, including comp etitors. These approaches are defensive 
in orientation: they aim to preserve trade secrets as long as possible while reserving patent 
rights, rather than to spring unfair surprises for their own sake. Becau se intentional delay 
arguments in equity (e.g., unclean hands or equitable estoppel) require misleading conduct 
and reliance by a specific adverse party, the mere use of Sub or UFO strategies—without 
targeting a competitor or making false assurances—is not, by itself, inequitable. 
Because the Submarine strategy is employe d primarily to protect the applicant’s 
own trade secrets, and because equitable defenses turn on case-specific facts (e.g., 
deception, reliance, prejudice), applicants who avoid targeted misleading acts and refrain 
from promising not to assert rights against sp ecific competitors should be secure against 
invalidation premised on “unclean hands.”  
 
C. Are Submarine Patents and UFO Designs Ethical? 

[PAGE 31]
 
31 
 
To dispose of the threshold concern: trade secret preservation is a legitimate policy 
choice. In Kewanee Oil Co. v. Bicron Corp., the Supreme Court held that state trade secret 
law is not preempted by the federal patent la ws and that trade secret protection coexists 
with, and does not undermine, federal patent policy encouraging invention. 22 Large 
institutions routinely preserve  trade secrets, and nothing in  American law suggests that 
choosing secrecy—particularly while reserving the right to patent in the United States—is 
unethical. The misgivings some  express about “submarine pate nts” generally reflect the 
pre-1996 landscape, not the “new” Submarine practice described here. 
To underscore: the “new” Submarine application is not subject to the old 17-year-
from-grant dynamic and does not  replicate the conduct that  triggered the prosecution-
laches line of cases against Lemelson-era patents. The modern framework—term measured 
from filing, publication rules, and statutory limits—obviates the core policy concerns that 
animated those decisions. 
 
D. Laches, Unclean Hands, and Equitable Estoppel: Practical Boundaries 
The statutory six-year look-back for dama ges cabins the remedial stakes and, as 
noted, laches cannot bar damages claims brought within that period. 23 Unclean hands and 
equitable estoppel remain available in appropriate circumstances, but they require specific 
proof (e.g., a misleading act, the opponent’s relia nce, or misconduct closely tied to the 
relief sought).  Consequently, where Sub and UFO strategies are used defensively—
without targeted misrepresent ations or promises to spec ific competitors—there is no 
inherent ethical or equitable defect. 
E. Takeaways 
Statutory Damages Window: Recovery of pa tent damages is limited by 35 U.S.C. 
§ 286; suits filed within that period are not barred by laches. 24 
Trade Secrets Are Legitimate. The Suprem e Court confirms that trade secret 
protection complements—not conflicts with—patent policy. 25 
Modern “Submarine” ≠ Old Abuses. The “new” U.S. Submarine strategy differs 
markedly from pre-1996 practices that triggered prosecution-laches rulings. 
Equity Is Fact-Specific. Absent mislead ing acts and reliance, equitable defenses 
such as unclean hands or estoppel do not convert lawful strategy into “gaming the system.” 
XV. Conclusions 
If your innovation is susceptible to trade secret protection—or if you are unwilling 
to accept 18-month publication—the prudent course  is to file in the United States first 

[PAGE 32]
 
32 
 
(effectively before any secret offer for sale ) and then follow up with a regular U.S. 
application accompanied by a non-publication request. Under U.S. law, when an applicant 
certifies upon filing that the invention has not been and wi ll not be the subject of any 
foreign or international filing that manda tes 18-month publication, the USPTO does not 
publish the application, thereby preserving tr ade secrecy while reserving U.S. patent 
rights.26 
Filing before any offer for sale—even one  made confidentially—is critical. The 
Supreme Court has made clear that a secret commercial sale to a third party may place the 
invention “on sale” and thus bar patenting if it predates the effective filing date, 
notwithstanding confidentiality. 27 Avoiding publication thr ough a valid non-publication 
request maximizes flexibility on when to en ter foreign systems a nd thus helps postpone 
deadline-driven cost explosions while reserving U.S. patent rights. 28 
Filing at least a U.S. Submarine application may furnish significant fallback 
protection should trade secrecy later be lost, because U.S. law recognizes trade secret 
protection as a legitimate, complementary incentive alongside patents; the two systems are 
not in conflict. 29

XVI. Definitions 
CIP (Continuation-in-Part) . A continuation-in-part pate nt application—only in the 
United States—that adds more technical improvement s to an earlier- filed application, 
while preserving appropriate priority to the earlier disclosure. 
CON (Continuation).  A continuation patent application—only in the United States—that 
does not add technical improvements and does not pursue patent claims that we subject to 
an election requirement. One could call UFO designs “UFO Cons” fo r this reason, but 
continuations are not understood internationally because they are unique to the U.S. 
CNIPA. The China National Intellectual Property Administration, China’s national patent 
office.

DDIV1, DDIV2, … DDIVN.  Divisional Design 1, Divisional Design 2, etc.  
D1, D2, … DN is a granted UFO design, Design 1, Design 2, etc. 
DIV (Divisional) .  A divisional patent applica tion that does no t add technical 
improvements and pursues patent claims that we not pursued in the parent, usually claims 
subject to an election requirement, but not in the case of design divisionals. 
EPO. The European Patent Office, the exa mining authority for European patent 
applications under the European Patent Convention. 
GUI. Graphical User Interface, i.e., the visual/ornamental aspects of user-facing screens or 
display elements. 
Mini-Sub.  A U.S. provisional application, useful for claiming priority without sacrificing 
the strategic option of filing a Submarine patent application. 
PCT. The Patent Cooperation Treaty, i.e., an in ternational patent application mechanism 
that defers national/regional filings and facilitates coordinated international prosecution. 
UFO Design. An “Unidentifiable Filed Ornamental” design, i.e., a “divisional” (actually a 
continuation) design application filed off a re gular U.S. patent ap plication to protect 
ornamental aspects (including physical articles and GUIs) reflected in integrated drawings. 
USPTO. The U.S. Patent & Trademark Office, the federal agency that examines and grants 
U.S. patents and designs and registers trademarks. 
RCE. A Request for Continued Examination, i. e., a renewed request for examination—
available only in the United States—to c ontinue prosecution of a pending utility 
application. 
SaaS. Software as a Service, i.e., online serv ices delivered from a server environment 
(essentially always behind a secure firewall). 
Submarine Application. A secret patent application, i.e ., a U.S. application filed with a 
non-publication request, availa ble only in the United Stat es, that remains unpublished 
unless and until it grants (or until the appl icant rescinds non-publication or triggers 
publication by foreign filing). 
WIPO. The World Intellectual Pr operty Organization, sometimes colloquially called the 
World Patent Office, which administers international IP systems (including the PCT).

Modular Smart Vending Cabinet with Smart-Glass Inspection Mode, Sensor-Fusion Manual Retrieval, and Quality-Based Dynamic Pricing

Field of the Invention
The present invention relates to autonomous retail cabinets and vending systems, and more particularly to a modular smart vending cabinet having a unified chassis supporting interchangeable dispensing and sensing modules, including privacy-first smart-glass inspection access for restricted goods, sensor-fusion based pay-as-you-pick charging for standard goods, and AI-driven revenue management for perishable and quality-sensitive products.
Background
Conventional vending machines and automated retail cabinets typically fall into one of several categories: (i) age-gated or ID-scanning dispensers that block dispensing until a credential is scanned; (ii) robotic vending cabinets that use a gantry or robotic retrieval mechanism; and (iii) dynamic pricing systems that apply discounts based on simple rules such as printed expiration dates or environmental conditions. These systems often do not provide a privacy-first, compliant user inspection experience for restricted goods, do not support a unified chassis that enables direct manual retrieval across multiple module types without robotics, and do not implement revenue-management algorithms that discount based on modeled sensory quality degradation and on slot-capacity yield (space yield) rather than only time-to-expiration.
Accordingly, there is a need for an integrated, hardware-anchored modular cabinet that (a) supports multiple dispensing modalities within a unified chassis; (b) enables direct manual retrieval monitored by a sensor-fusion network (rather than robotic retrieval); (c) implements a privacy-first restricted-goods workflow using smart glass and a digital information display prior to access; and (d) applies dynamic pricing based on sensory decay modeling and space-yield displacement to reduce waste and increase revenue.

Detailed Description
In one embodiment, and with reference to FIG. 1, a universal smart cabinet system comprises a secure enclosure having an interior volume accessible through at least one door, an access-control mechanism coupled to the door, a unified reconfigurable chassis arranged within the interior volume, an exterior user interface, and one or more cameras mounted on or near the front of the cabinet. The universal smart cabinet may be implemented as one or more sealed unit types including (i) a secure unit for restricted goods, (ii) an ambient unit, and (iii) a chilled unit (e.g., refrigerated), each sharing the same chassis architecture and sensor-fusion network but operating under different access-control and transaction state machines. The exterior user interface may include a touch-sensitive display and/or physical buttons that allow a user to authenticate, request cabinet access, view pricing information, and confirm purchase operations. In secure mode embodiments, the cabinet further includes a biometric camera (also referred to as “The Eye”) positioned to capture facial image data of users in front of the cabinet and, in some implementations, also observe an interior region near the front of the chassis modules. In embodiments, the front edge of one or more modules defines a linear “front zone” region where a corresponding antenna matrix is positioned, as generally illustrated in FIG. 1.
With reference to FIG. 2, the system further includes a processing system implemented as an edge controller or edge AI unit located in an electronics bay within or adjacent to the cabinet. The edge controller includes at least one processor, memory, and one or more machine-readable instruction sets that implement a pricing engine, an inventory-valuation module, a biometric analysis module, an access-control module, a transaction-processing module, and a sensor-fusion module. The edge controller is communicatively coupled to the various sensors (including the cameras, RFID antenna matrix, and, where installed, load cells), to the access-control mechanism, and, when available, to a remote server via a network interface (e.g., LTE, Wi-Fi, or Ethernet). As shown schematically in FIG. 2, the edge controller maintains a local cache or database of inventory, pricing, user deposit, and transaction data, and may be powered in part by a backup or uninterruptible power supply so that core functions can continue during power or network disruptions. In particular, basic functions such as unlocking and locking the door, executing the cabinet state machine, detecting removals, updating inventory, and debiting funds against a locally maintained deposit or balance can be carried out autonomously via local caching on the edge controller even when a cloud AI service or external network connection is unavailable, thereby preventing the cabinet from becoming inoperative in the event of an internet outage.
With reference to FIG. 3, the unified chassis is configured to simultaneously support multiple interchangeable mechanical modules for direct manual retrieval, including at least: (i) smart pushers for bottles/cartons, (ii) smart hooks for bagged goods, (iii) box-on-scale zones for soft or fragile items, and (iv) gated gravity-feed channels for small or low-cost items. In an example configuration, a pusher module comprises spring-loaded tracks and movable dividers (adjustable width) configured to advance products toward a front retrieval edge, and the front edge includes an antenna matrix configured for virtual zoning and tag tracking. A hook module comprises hanging rods or hooks that are configured for rear loading and direct manual removal from a front access area. A box-on-scale module comprises a designated box or zone supported by at least one load cell, where removal is confirmed by a weight delta and corroborated by camera evidence, thereby accommodating items not well-suited to compression by springs (e.g., milk pouches, mayonnaise jars, coffee beans in boxes) and enabling multi-picking during a session. A gated gravity-feed module comprises an inclined shelf or chute configured to deliver items to a bottom weighing dish supported by a load cell and includes a controllable gate for sequential release.
In embodiments, the system enforces FIFO loading and rotation as part of an operational workflow. With reference to FIG. 4, the system includes an induction station used for associating product information with internal tracking tags prior to stocking the cabinet. The induction station may be embodied as a fixed docking station or as a handheld scanner device that is communicatively coupled to the edge controller. In an example induction workflow, staff scan a manufacturer barcode or SKU code to register an item, and then apply an internal tracking tag (e.g., RFID, NFC, or a machine-readable sticker) that is associated in a database with the SKU and, where applicable, a lot/batch identifier and an expiration date or production date. The cabinet is configured for rear loading such that new stock is inserted from the back of a module and pushes existing stock toward the front retrieval edge, thereby ensuring that the first item loaded is the first item sold (first-in-first-out). This pre-association and rear-loading configuration ensures that, when a tagged item is later detected by the antenna matrix and/or other sensors, the edge controller can resolve product identity and related metadata from the tag association.
By combining the pre-association performed at the induction station (FIG. 4) with per-module sensing and camera overlap (FIG. 3), the system maintains an accurate item-level and/or position-level view of inventory and transactions. Unlike robotic vending cabinets that rely on gantries or robotic retrieval mechanisms, the claimed system supports direct manual retrieval and uses a unified sensor-fusion region to confirm removal events across module types. In embodiments, the sensor-fusion module combines at least two of: (i) RFID readings from the antenna matrix or segmented near-field antennas, (ii) camera detections of hand entry, product motion, and removal gestures, (iii) weight delta measurements from load cells in box-on-scale zones and/or weighing dishes, and (iv) door state and time-window logic. For example, if the camera detects that a user’s hand has removed an object from a particular zone within the overlap region but the corresponding RFID antennas do not detect removal of an expected tag (e.g., due to RF shielding by foil packaging), the system treats this as an anomalous event. In response, the edge controller may trigger an audible or visual alarm, temporarily restrict access, hold or increase a user deposit, flag the session for review, or otherwise apply anti-fraud controls.
In embodiments employing the gated gravity-feed module, the system implements a gated gravity sequencing logic in which release of a next item is dependent on confirmed removal of a prior item. In one implementation, a first item sits in a weighing dish at the bottom of the chute and the gate remains locked while the load cell detects the presence of the item (weight above a threshold). When the user removes the first item, the system detects the weight returning to zero (or below a removal threshold) and corroborates removal via camera identification of the item and/or the removal gesture. Only after the removal is confirmed does the edge controller actuate the gate to release a second item into the weighing dish, after which the gate is again locked until the second item is removed. This gating logic prevents sweeping multiple items without detection and enables accurate per-item charging in low-cost or high-velocity item lanes.
In one embodiment, the universal smart cabinet operates under multiple logic flows depending on unit type. In an embodiment directed to restricted goods such as alcohol (Unit A: Secure), the system operates in a restricted-mode configuration that includes an opaque smart glass door panel and a “digital sommelier” user experience. In such embodiments, the access-control mechanism remains locked until completion of a biometric age-verification protocol and a payment commitment (e.g., a pre-authorization hold). The smart glass defaults to an opaque state to enforce privacy and prevent visual access to restricted goods. When a user approaches the cabinet, the biometric camera captures facial image data and the biometric analysis module executed on the edge controller runs one or more models to estimate age and/or match the face to a verified identity associated with a user account or backend KYC database. Upon successful verification (e.g., age greater than or equal to a legal minimum age such as twenty-one years, optionally with confidence above a threshold), the system enters an inspection mode in which the smart glass transitions from opaque to transparent and the display presents product information (e.g., vintage, tasting notes, origin, or pairing suggestions) prior to granting physical access. Following inspection mode, the transaction-processing module initiates a payment commitment logic flow, such as placing a pre-authorization hold or deposit, optionally based on a risk profile. Only upon confirmation of the payment commitment does the access-control module release the door to permit manual retrieval. Upon removal, the sensor-fusion module confirms the removed item(s), and the system captures the corresponding charge, releasing any remaining pre-authorization when applicable.
In an embodiment directed to ambient and chilled sealed units (Unit B: Standard), the system uses a transparent door panel and a tap-to-pay or equivalent authorization flow. Upon receiving a payment authorization, the access-control mechanism unlocks and the system operates in a pay-as-you-pick configuration in which each item removal is detected by sensor fusion (e.g., RFID plus camera, and load-cell deltas where present) and charged immediately or near-immediately. The user interface may provide a digital label function in which product information and pricing (including discounts) are displayed and can be zoomed or browsed during the session.
In embodiments, the pricing engine implements active revenue management algorithms that go beyond discounts tied solely to printed expiration dates. In one embodiment, the pricing engine implements a sensory decay algorithm that models quality loss occurring before expiration. The system stores, for each SKU or product category, one or more parameters representing predicted loss of sensory appeal (e.g., chocolate blooming, dried fruit clumping, staling, oxidation, moisture-driven texture changes) that may occur prior to the expiration date while the product remains safe to consume. The sensory decay model may use inputs such as time since stocking, lot/batch data, ambient temperature or humidity readings, handling patterns inferred from transactions, and/or visual appearance indicators derived from camera analysis. When the model predicts that continuing to offer the product at full price is likely to reduce sell-through due to diminished sensory appeal, the pricing engine triggers an accelerated discount schedule prior to expiry.
In a further embodiment, the pricing engine implements a space yield displacement algorithm that discounts items to free physical slot capacity based on turnover rather than only remaining shelf life. The system computes a revenue-per-slot (or revenue-per-bay) metric over a time window using inventory and transaction logs. If a product remains fresh but occupies a slot with low turnover relative to alternatives, the pricing engine automatically lowers the price to force an exit and free the slot for new, higher-velocity inventory, thereby optimizing physical slot utilization and cabinet throughput. Dynamic pricing decisions can be computed locally by the edge controller so that, even if cloud-based analytics are temporarily unavailable, the system continues to apply and display appropriate discounts based on locally cached rules and data. Updated prices may be stored in item-level or SKU-level pricing records and communicated to the user interface and, when connectivity exists, to a remote management portal. To guide users to discounted items, the system may activate an addressable indicator (e.g., LEDs) associated with a corresponding shelf zone or module position.
The system can support mixed-mode transactions in which restricted goods and ambient/chilled goods are present in the same cabinet or in adjacent sealed units sharing a common controller, with operating mode, payment logic, biometric requirements, and sensor-fusion thresholds tailored based on the unit type and items enabled for sale. A mode state may be stored in the processing system and optionally controlled by a remote management server. This multi-modal behavior may be implemented in software as a state machine that, in each state, configures device drivers, module sensing logic, pricing rules (including sensory decay and space yield), biometric requirements, payment commitment thresholds, and offline/online behaviors accordingly. Because the claimed system is anchored in specific physical components, as illustrated in FIGS. 1–4—including the secure enclosure with one or more sealed units, the unified reconfigurable chassis supporting interchangeable modules, the induction station for associating SKU and tracking data with internal tags, antenna matrices and/or segmented RFID antennas, camera-based sensor-fusion geometry, load cells for designated zones and weighing dishes, the edge controller with local caching, and access-control hardware—and implements concrete technical logic for sensor fusion, gated gravity sequencing, dynamic pricing based on sensory decay and space yield displacement, offline transaction handling, and restricted-goods smart-glass inspection access, it provides technical improvements over generic retail computers, including improved fraud resistance without robotics, improved inventory rotation, increased sell-through with reduced waste, and resilience to network outages.

Abstract
A modular smart vending cabinet system includes a secure enclosure defining an interior volume accessible through a lockable door, a reconfigurable unified chassis supporting a plurality of interchangeable product modules operable simultaneously, and an edge controller executing sensor-fusion, access-control, transaction, inventory, and pricing logic. The chassis supports at least a pusher module with adjustable dividers and an antenna matrix, a hook module configured for rear loading, a box-on-scale module using load-cell weight deltas corroborated by camera evidence for fragile items, and a gated gravity-feed module in which a gate releases a next item to a weighing dish only after confirmed removal of a prior item. The system performs staff induction and tagging by associating a scanned SKU with an internal tracking tag and supports FIFO rear loading to advance older inventory to a front retrieval edge. In a restricted-goods mode, an opaque smart-glass door provides privacy and transitions to a transparent inspection mode after biometric verification to present product information prior to access, and access is unlocked following payment commitment. In standard ambient or chilled modes, the system unlocks upon payment authorization and performs pay-as-you-pick charging based on sensor-fused detection of manual removals. A pricing engine applies dynamic discounts based on sensory decay modeling of quality loss prior to expiration and on space-yield displacement to free low-turnover slot capacity.

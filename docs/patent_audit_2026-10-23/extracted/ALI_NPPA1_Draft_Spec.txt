FIELD
[0002] The present disclosure relates to autonomous and unattended retail systems. More particularly, the disclosure relates to modular retail cabinets having a reconfigurable chassis that supplies power and data to interchangeable merchandising and processing modules at selectable positions, an active leveling system, a sensor network that confirms direct manual retrieval of goods by fusing imaging with module-specific item-detection sensing, controlled user access including tokenized, biometric, and restricted-goods workflows, and thermal and fluid processing modules that dock to the same chassis.
BACKGROUND
[0003] Conventional vending machines and grab-and-go cabinets generally use a fixed internal layout. A coil, pusher, or shelf arrangement chosen at the factory is difficult to change in the field. Moving a powered or sensed shelf to a new height typically requires rewiring or a service visit, so an operator cannot readily adapt a cabinet to a changing product mix.
[0004] Many automated cabinets rely on a single detection technology. Radio-frequency identification (RFID) systems may miss tags shielded by foil packaging or liquids. Weight-only systems are sensitive to vibration, door impacts, and the inclination of the cabinet on an uneven floor. Camera-only systems can be confused by occlusion. Robotic gantry systems avoid some of these problems but add cost, slow the transaction, and limit the kinds of goods that can be offered.
[0005] Gravity-fed shelves, spring pushers, and load cells all assume that the cabinet stands level. A cabinet that is moved for cleaning or restocking, or that is installed on a sloped floor, may feed items unreliably and may report weight changes that do not correspond to actual removals. This creates both service failures and opportunities for fraud.
[0006] Existing cabinets also handle access control and payment in ways that invite disputes. Transaction terms are often not presented before access is granted, returns are difficult to police, and restricted goods such as alcohol or tobacco are commonly gated by an identification scan without a privacy-preserving inspection experience. Many cabinets stop working entirely when an internet connection or external power is lost.
[0007] Finally, operators increasingly want a single cabinet platform to serve chilled, ambient, and prepared foods. Heating food inside an enclosure that also holds chilled stock introduces moisture, odors, and condensation that degrade the stored goods. Heated items that are paid for but not collected create sanitation and waste problems. Conventional systems treat heating and beverage preparation as separate machines rather than as modules of a common chassis.
[0008] There remains a need for a modular autonomous retail platform in which merchandising and processing modules of different types share one reconfigurable chassis, the cabinet maintains its own level, item removal is confirmed by sensing appropriate to each module, access is controlled in an auditable way, and thermal and fluid processing can be added without compromising chilled storage.
SUMMARY
[0009] This summary introduces concepts that are further described in the detailed description. It is not intended to identify key or essential features of the claimed subject matter or to limit its scope.
[0010] In one aspect, an autonomous retail cabinet includes an enclosure with a lockable user access barrier and a chassis, sometimes called a "transformer base," that defines a plurality of mounting positions spaced along a vertical direction. A position-adjustable interface extends along the chassis and supplies electrical power and data at each mounting position, so that a module can be moved from one height to another without disconnecting or re-terminating wiring. The interface may be a conductive busbar with sliding contacts or inductive couplers, a drag-chain cable carrier, or a flexible harness forming a service loop.
[0011] Interchangeable modules of different types mount at the mounting positions. Each module has a connector that couples to the interface and a module identifier readable over the interface. Module types include a spring pusher module, a hook module, a scale-box or smart-bin module, a gated gravity-feed module, a gravity tray module, a sliding tray module, an auto-purge module, a thermal processing module, and a fluid processing module. Different modules carry different item-detection sensors, such as an RFID antenna matrix, a load cell, or an optical break-beam sensor.
[0012] An active leveling system includes motorized leveling feet and a tilt sensor coupled to the chassis. A controller drives the feet to reduce tilt when the tilt exceeds a threshold, for example about 0.5 degrees, and inhibits initiation of a user session, heating, or fluid dispensing while the tilt remains above the threshold. Leveling keeps load-cell readings accurate, keeps gravity-fed and spring-fed modules feeding reliably, and keeps liquids level during heating.
[0013] When a module is mounted, the controller reads its identifier over the interface and associates with that mounting position a detection profile corresponding to the module type. At least one internal camera views the product presentation regions of the modules. The controller confirms direct manual retrieval of an item by fusing the camera output with the output of the item-detection sensor that the detection profile selects. Removal is therefore confirmed without a robotic gantry, using sensing suited to each module.
[0014] In further aspects, a gated gravity-feed module releases the next item into a weighing dish only after removal of the prior item is confirmed. A restricted-goods compartment has a switchable smart-glass panel that stays opaque until an age requirement is satisfied, becomes transparent for inspection, and unlocks only after a payment commitment, with removal confirmed by antenna, camera, and load-cell fusion. The cabinet may present transaction terms and require affirmative consent before unlocking, may maintain a local ledger and backup power to operate through network and power outages, and may operate as a master unit controlling one or more slave units under a single virtual basket.
[0015] In another aspect, a method of operating such a cabinet includes detecting a mounted module and reading its identifier, associating a detection profile with its mounting position, leveling the cabinet and inhibiting user sessions while it is out of level, authenticating a user and unlocking the access barrier, and confirming item removal by fusing camera images with the item-detection sensor identified by the detection profile.
[0016] In yet another aspect, a thermal processing module mounts at a mounting position of the chassis and receives power and data from the interface. The module includes a hermetically sealable heating chamber, an induction coil that heats a susceptor-bearing package, an identifier reader, a negative-pressure ventilation system that draws steam and odors out of the chamber during heating, and a lockable retrieval door. Heating is conditioned on a recorded payment commitment and follows a heating profile associated with the package identifier. If the heated item is not retrieved before a retrieval timer expires, the retrieval door is locked and the item is diverted to a waste compartment, which may be held below about −2 °C.

A. Cabinet overview
[0041] Referring to FIG. 1, an autonomous retail cabinet 100 includes an enclosure 102 that defines a storage volume 104. A user access barrier 106, such as a hinged or sliding front door, closes the storage volume 104. The user access barrier 106 may include a transparent panel 108. An electronic lock 112 holds the user access barrier 106 in a locked state and releases it to an unlocked state under control of an edge controller 400 (FIG. 2). A mechanical key override 114 permits authorized maintenance access when the electronics are unavailable. A door status sensor 116, such as a magnetic reed switch, reports whether the user access barrier 106 is open or closed. An alarm 118, which may be a buzzer or audio speaker, provides audible feedback and alerts.
[0042] A user interface display 120, such as a touch-sensitive display or tablet, presents prompts, pricing, transaction terms, inventory information, and instructions. In some embodiments the user interface display 120 is integrated into or mounted on the user access barrier 106. In other embodiments it is mounted on an articulating boom arm or a side bracket that allows its height and angle to be adjusted. An external biometric camera 122 is mounted on an exterior surface of the enclosure 102, a door bezel, or an adjacent kiosk structure, and is oriented to capture facial imagery of a user standing in front of the cabinet 100. An optical code scanner 124 reads a QR code or barcode presented on a user's mobile device. A payment reader 126 accepts NFC credentials, contactless cards, or other payment instruments.
[0043] The cabinet 100 may be configured as one or more sealed unit types sharing the same chassis architecture and sensor network but operating under different access-control and transaction state machines. Examples include a secure unit for restricted goods, an ambient unit, and a chilled (refrigerated) unit. A single cabinet may also include a restricted-goods compartment 500 alongside standard compartments, as described below with reference to FIGS. 12A and 12B.
B. Reconfigurable chassis and position-adjustable interface
[0044] Referring to FIG. 2, a chassis 200 is arranged within the storage volume 104. The chassis 200 includes at least one vertical upright 202, which may be located along a rear wall of the enclosure 102 or along a side wall. The chassis 200 defines a plurality of bays 204 and a plurality of mounting positions 206 spaced along a vertical direction. Module supports 208, such as rails, brackets, or slots, hold an interchangeable module 300 at any selected mounting position 206. Because the chassis 200 can be rearranged quickly to suit different product mixes, it is sometimes referred to as a "transformer base."
[0045] A position-adjustable interface 210 extends along the vertical upright 202 or another structural member of the chassis 200. The position-adjustable interface 210 supplies electrical power, data, or both to a module 300 at each of the mounting positions 206. A module 300 can be moved from one mounting position 206 to another, at discrete or continuous heights, without disconnecting and re-terminating wiring. Each module 300 includes a module connector 308 that couples to the position-adjustable interface 210 and a module identifier 309, such as an identification chip or stored configuration record, that the edge controller 400 can read over the interface. The interface supports hot-swap identification of a newly docked module and bidirectional communication between the module and the edge controller 400. In some embodiments, control signals are exchanged over a wired bus carried by the interface, over a short-range wireless link between the edge controller 400 and the module, or both.
[0046] FIGS. 3A to 3C show three non-limiting implementations of the position-adjustable interface 210. In FIG. 3A, the interface is a conductive busbar having vertical conductive rails 212. The module connector 308 carries sliding contacts 214 that engage the rails 212 at whatever mounting position 206 the module occupies. Instead of or in addition to galvanic contacts, the module connector 308 may include an inductive coupler 215 that transfers power, data, or both across a small gap. In FIG. 3B, the interface is a drag-chain cable carrier 216, an articulated track that guides one or more conductors 217 between the electronics bay 140 and the module as the module is repositioned. In FIG. 3C, the interface is a flexible harness 218 with a service loop 219 that has enough slack to allow vertical repositioning while maintaining electrical continuity. In each case, the interface allows rapid reconfiguration while maintaining a defined power and data coupling between the module and the cabinet electronics. The interface may also be implemented as a cable chain, a suspended loop, or another distribution structure that permits modules to be rearranged without custom wiring.
[0047] Referring again to FIG. 2, the enclosure 102 provides dual access. Users access goods from the front through the user access barrier 106. Operators load goods from the rear through a rear service door 110 or rear access panel. Rear loading pushes existing stock toward the front, so the first item loaded is the first item sold (first-in, first-out, or FIFO). The rear-loading arrangement also supports pass-through installations, such as wall-mounted or walk-in-cooler deployments.
[0048] An electronics bay 140 houses the edge controller 400, a network interface 406, and an uninterruptible power supply (UPS) or battery unit 142. At least one internal camera 450 is mounted within the storage volume 104, for example overhead or on a side wall, with a field of view 452 that includes the product presentation regions of the modules 300.
C. Mobility and active leveling
[0049] Referring to FIGS. 2 and 4, a mobility system 130 includes industrial casters 132 that allow the cabinet 100 to be rolled away from a wall or fixed position. This gives an operator access to the rear service door 110 for FIFO stocking, cleaning, and maintenance. Retractable leveling feet engage the floor to stabilize the cabinet during use.
[0050] In some embodiments the leveling feet are motorized leveling feet 134 forming part of an active leveling system. Each motorized leveling foot 134 includes an electric actuator 136, such as a screw-drive actuator, and a foot pad 137 that contacts the floor. A tilt sensor 138, such as an inclinometer, gyroscopic sensor, or inertial measurement unit, is mounted to the chassis 200 and continuously outputs a tilt signal representing the inclination of the chassis relative to true horizontal. A leveling module 416 of the edge controller 400 receives the tilt signal and drives the actuators 136.
[0051] FIG. 5 shows an example leveling routine. At step S101 the controller reads the tilt signal. At step S102 it compares the tilt to a calibration threshold, for example about 0.5 degrees from true horizontal. If the tilt is within the threshold, the controller enables user sessions and processing operations (step S103). If the tilt exceeds the threshold, the controller inhibits initiation of a user session or transaction (step S104) and drives the actuators 136 to bring the chassis back toward level (step S105). It then re-reads the tilt (step S106). If leveling succeeds, sessions are enabled again. If the tilt cannot be brought within the threshold after a set number of attempts or a set time, the controller keeps sessions inhibited and sends a service alert (step S107). The routine may run at startup, periodically, after the cabinet is moved, and on detection of abnormal movement or vibration (step S108).
[0052] Active leveling provides several technical benefits. First, it maintains a stable reference plane for load-cell measurements, improving their accuracy and reducing false or missed removal events that could be exploited for fraud. Second, gravity-fed shelves and spring-loaded pushers feed reliably only when their incline is as designed, so leveling improves mechanical reliability. Third, as described with reference to FIG. 16, leveling keeps the surface of a liquid level during heating under a sealed film, reducing hot spots and improving heating uniformity. In some embodiments, the controller also inhibits heating initiation and fluid dispensing while the tilt exceeds the threshold, and re-levels before enabling those operations.
D. Interchangeable modules
[0053] Several types of module 300 may be mounted on the chassis 200 at the same time, and each may be inserted, removed, and repositioned. Each module may include local sensing and actuation, such as gates, latches, motors, load cells, break-beam sensors, RFID antennas, and temperature sensors, that report to the edge controller 400 through the position-adjustable interface 210. For convenience, module types are designated A through G below. The thermal processing module 600 and fluid processing module 700, described in Section L, are further module types that dock to the same chassis.
[0054] Module A, the pusher module 310 (FIG. 6A), includes a track 318, a spring-loaded pusher 312 that advances products toward a user-facing retrieval edge, and adjustable dividers 314 that set lane width. It suits rigid packages such as bottles and cartons. A one-way ratchet 316 or pawl prevents a product from being pushed back toward the rear once it has advanced past a removal threshold.
[0055] Module B, the hook module 320 (FIG. 6B), includes hooks or rods 322 that support hanging products. It is loaded from the rear and products are removed manually from the front. A stop or pawl 324 may inhibit rehanging of a removed product.
[0056] Module C, the scale-box module 330 (FIG. 6C), includes a receptacle 332 supported by at least one load cell 334. Removal is detected as a decrease in weight and corroborated by camera evidence. It suits items that should not be compressed by springs, such as milk pouches, jars, or boxed coffee beans, and items sold by weight. It also allows a user to pick several items during a session. In a smart-bin variant, amorphous goods such as flexible milk bags are debited according to the weight lost.
[0057] Module D, the gated gravity-feed module 340 (FIG. 7), includes an inclined chute 342 that delivers items by gravity toward a controllable gate 344. A gate actuator 346 opens and closes the gate. Below or in front of the gate is a weighing dish 348 supported by a dish load cell 349. The gate releases one item at a time into the weighing dish, as described with reference to FIG. 11. This module suits small, low-cost, or high-velocity items.
[0058] Module E, the gravity tray module 350 (FIG. 8A), includes an inclined low-friction surface 352 that presents delicate trays, such as meat or salad trays, and advances the next tray forward by gravity. One or more load cells 354 confirm removals. A temperature sensor 356 is coupled to the edge controller 400, which implements a temperature interlock: if the sensed shelf temperature exceeds a safety limit, the controller inhibits sale of the affected slot, inhibits unlocking for that slot, or both.
[0059] Module F, the sliding tray module 360 (FIG. 6D), includes a low-friction floor 362 that slopes toward a retrieval opening 364. Products advance by gravity or by user movement rather than by spring compression. It suits soft or deformable packaging, such as meat, cheese, and deli items, that a spring pusher would damage.
[0060] Module G, the auto-purge module 370 (FIG. 8B), includes a retractable front stop 372 movable by a stop actuator 374 between a retain position and a purge position. Section G below describes its operation.
[0061] A floating smart bin 390, shown in FIG. 21A and described in Section M, is a further weighing module for soft packaging.
E. Smart edge, sensing geometry, and sensor fusion
[0062] Referring to FIGS. 1 and 9, each module 300 may include a smart edge 302 along its user-facing front portion. The smart edge 302 includes a high-density antenna matrix 304, meaning an array of antenna elements 305 rather than a single antenna, and a shelf-edge display 306. The antenna matrix 304 reads tags 464 on product items 462 in defined pick zones and supports virtual zoning, so that a tag read can be localized to a lane or slot. The front edge of the modules thus defines a linear "front zone" monitored by the antenna matrix. The antenna matrix may be replaced or supplemented by segmented near-field RFID antennas, NFC readers, or drawer-mounted antenna arrays.
[0063] The shelf-edge display 306 may be an addressable LED information strip, an electronic shelf label (ESL), an LCD strip, or a full-width video rail. It provides navigation and pricing cues, including per-item indicators, guidance toward discounted items, and session prompts. In some embodiments it displays alphanumeric data, including a real-time dynamic price and batch metadata, such as a lot identifier and an expiration date, for the specific item at the front of the lane. The edge controller 400 maintains a virtual FIFO queue of expiration timestamps that mirrors the physical loading sequence of a lane. When it detects removal of the front item, it automatically updates the shelf-edge display 306 to show the price and expiration date of the next item in the queue. In some embodiments the display shows advertising or brand content in an idle mode and switches to a proximity mode, showing price, weight, and expiration date, when a user is detected near the shelf. It may also show a warning when an expiration date is near.
[0064] The internal camera 450 forms a continuous visual tracking layer that tracks hand movement, item interaction, and product movement within the shelf regions. Its field of view 452 overlaps the front zone of the antenna matrix 304, defining an overlap region 454 used for sensor fusion. A camera may also be mounted near the front of the cabinet so that it observes both the user and the front of the modules.
[0065] In addition to the visual tracking layer, the edge controller applies a selectable physical detection layer to each zone. In a high-value zone 458, the physical detection layer includes load cells and RFID sensing through the antenna matrix 304. In a standard zone 460, it includes an optical break-beam sensor 456 and a mechanical anti-reverse inhibitor, such as the one-way ratchet 316, without requiring load cells. Other zones may use any combination of RFID, weight, break-beam, door-state, proximity, and time-window sensing.
[0066] The physical detection layer for each mounting position 206 is set by a detection profile. When a module 300 docks, a module manager 414 of the edge controller 400 reads its module identifier 309 over the position-adjustable interface 210, determines its module type, and associates with that mounting position a detection profile that identifies which item-detection sensors are present, how their outputs are to be interpreted, and what thresholds apply. Moving a module to a new position moves its detection profile with it, without manual reconfiguration.
[0067] FIG. 10 shows an example sensor-fusion routine executed by a sensor-fusion module 412. At step S201 the controller detects an interaction event, such as a hand entering the overlap region 454 of a zone. At step S202 it retrieves the detection profile for that zone. At step S203 it collects the camera output and the output of each item-detection sensor named in the profile, such as a change in tag reads, a load-cell delta, or a break-beam interruption, within a time window. At step S204 it determines whether the camera and the item-detection sensors agree that a specific item was removed. If they agree, the controller records the removal against the user session and updates inventory (step S205). If the camera detects that a hand removed an object but the expected item-detection signal is absent, for example because foil packaging shielded an RFID tag, the controller treats the event as anomalous (step S206). In response it may sound the alarm 118, temporarily restrict access, hold or increase a user deposit, flag the session for review, or apply another anti-fraud control (step S207). Door state and time-window logic may serve as further fusion inputs.
[0068] Because removal is confirmed by sensor fusion across module types, the cabinet supports direct manual retrieval without a robotic gantry. The combination of modules, per-zone detection profiles, and a shared visual tracking layer improves fraud resistance at lower cost than robotic retrieval.
[0069] FIG. 11 shows a gated gravity sequencing routine for the gated gravity-feed module 340. At step S301 the gate 344 releases a first item into the weighing dish 348. At step S302 the controller locks the gate while the dish load cell 349 reports a weight above a presence threshold. At step S303 it monitors for the weight to fall below a removal threshold. At step S304 it corroborates the removal using the internal camera 450, by identifying the item or the removal gesture. Only after removal is confirmed does the controller record the sale (step S305) and actuate the gate to release the next item (step S306). The gate is then locked again until that item is removed. This sequencing prevents a user from sweeping several items at once and allows accurate per-item charging.
F. Sales-final policy and return handling
[0070] In some embodiments, the cabinet enforces a sales-final ("no return") policy using mechanical anti-reverse elements, software return detection, or both. Mechanically, ratchets, pawls, stops, or directional teeth in pushers, hooks, gravity-feed mechanisms, and profiled guide channels physically inhibit backward movement once an item has advanced past a removal threshold. In software, for zones without such elements, such as bins and sliding trays, the edge controller executes return-detection logic using load-cell data, break-beam data, RFID reads, and the visual tracking layer.
[0071] When the controller detects a weight increase or other signal indicating a return attempt during an active session that is not validated by computer vision, it applies a penalty. In various embodiments the penalty is a second charge at the current unit price (a double charge), a charge equal to a multiple of the highest-value item currently stocked in the enclosure or in the affected zone, or another configured fee. Before unlocking, the user interface display 120 presents the no-return terms and penalty logic and requires an affirmative input, such as selection of an "I Agree" control. The controller stores a time-stamped consent record in the local ledger 408 or a cloud ledger and associates it with the session identifier and transaction record. Recording assent before access reduces chargeback risk and provides an auditable record.
G. Auto-purge and smart waste
[0072] Referring to FIG. 8B, the auto-purge module 370 clears expired or quarantined items to free slot capacity. When a purge condition occurs, such as a scheduled purge time, an operator command, or an expired status, the controller moves the retractable front stop 372 to its purge position. A spring 376 or gravity then advances the retained item forward until it drops into a smart waste compartment 380 at the bottom of the cabinet. The smart waste compartment 380 includes a waste load cell 382 and a waste antenna 384. Items in it are tracked as "quarantined," not deleted, pending staff collection. To discourage scavenging, the controller may apply an anti-scavenger rule that charges the item's full original price if a user removes an item from the smart waste compartment.
H. Restricted-goods compartment
[0073] Referring to FIGS. 12A and 12B, a restricted-goods compartment 500 holds goods such as alcohol or tobacco. It has a compartment door 504, a compartment lock 506, and a switchable smart-glass panel 502, such as a polymer-dispersed liquid crystal (PDLC) panel. The panel has an opaque sleep state (FIG. 12A), which preserves privacy and prevents visual access to the restricted goods, and a transparent inspection state (FIG. 12B). Restricted goods are monitored by a mandatory triple sensor fusion of the antenna matrix 304, an overhead or interior-facing camera 450, and load cells 508 under the restricted-goods zone. The load cells allow the controller to detect partial consumption, such as opening a bottle and returning it, and to resist tag swapping and "ghost" returns that RFID alone may not detect.
[0074] In a restricted-goods mode, the panel 502 remains opaque until the user satisfies an age requirement. Age may be verified biometrically: the external biometric camera 122 captures facial image data, and a biometric analysis module 426 estimates the user's age, matches the face to a verified identity, or both, optionally with a confidence above a threshold. Age may also be verified by token: scanning an optical code from an account whose age attribute was verified through a know-your-customer (KYC) process may be sufficient, without mandatory camera capture, in some jurisdictions or configurations. After verification, the controller switches the panel to its transparent inspection state, and the display presents product information, such as vintage, tasting notes, origin, or pairing suggestions (a "digital sommelier" experience). The controller then requests a payment commitment, such as a pre-authorization hold or deposit, whose amount may depend on a risk profile based on transaction history, chargeback history, or product category. Only after the commitment is approved does it unlock the compartment door 504. On removal, the triple sensor fusion confirms the item, the charge is captured, and any unused pre-authorization is released.
[0075] A cabinet or compartment may switch dynamically between a standard mode and the restricted-goods mode without hardware changes. The switch may be triggered by the detected product type, a time-of-day rule (for example, an automatic lockout after a specified hour), or a remote command. In the standard mode, the access barrier unlocks on payment authorization alone and the biometric step is bypassed. Mode state is held in the edge controller and may be controlled by a remote management server.
I. User sessions and access control
[0076] FIG. 13 shows an example user access and session routine executed by an access-control module 410 and a transaction module 420. At step S401 the controller establishes a user session. The session may be established by decoding a session token from an optical code generated by a mobile application on the user's mobile device 902 and read by the optical code scanner 124, by biometric identification using the external biometric camera 122, or by other factors, including palm or fingerprint recognition, an NFC credential, a card, or a mobile-application credential. Biometric checks may include anti-spoofing measures. The token links to a user profile and one or more payment instruments.
[0077] At step S402 the controller reserves funds by initiating a pre-authorization based on the token or payment instrument. It may verify that the identity associated with the payment instrument matches the registered user profile, for example by KYC or issuer verification. At step S403 it displays the transaction terms, such as "immediate charge upon removal," "incremental hold," and "no returns," together with any penalty logic, and at step S404 it requires an affirmative consent input. At step S405, if restricted goods are requested, it applies the restricted-goods mode of Section H. At step S406 it checks that the cabinet is level and that sessions are enabled. At step S407 it unlocks the user access barrier 106 and may illuminate the authorized region using the shelf-edge display 306.
[0078] During the session, the controller maintains a virtual basket. For each removal confirmed by sensor fusion (step S408), the controller adds the item to the basket and applies a real-time incremental authorization (step S409), as described in Section O. At step S410 the session ends, for example on door closure, a checkout input, a timeout, or the user's departure from a cluster of cabinets. The controller captures the funds, generates a digital receipt and, where local tax rules require it, a fiscal receipt, and sends the receipt to the mobile application or a registered email address (step S411).
[0079] In some embodiments, biometric enrollment is progressive. A user's first session at a cabinet is authorized by a token. The cabinet then captures a facial vector or other biometric vector and requests explicit opt-in consent to enrollment. N. Pricing engine
[0109] The pricing engine 418 runs on the edge controller 400, so it keeps applying and displaying prices from locally cached rules and data when cloud analytics are unavailable. Updated prices are stored in item-level or SKU-level pricing records, published to the shelf-edge displays 306 and the user interface display 120, and synchronized with a remote management portal when a connection is available. Pricing decisions and their inputs may be stored in an auditable log. To guide users to discounted items, the controller may light the shelf-edge display of the corresponding zone in a distinct color or pattern. Pricing rules may apply to food and non-food goods, perishable and non-perishable.
[0110] The pricing engine may apply any combination of the following algorithms. (a) Time-based decay: a standard discount curve tied to time remaining until expiration, subject to a minimum price. (b) Sensory decay: per-SKU or per-category parameters model loss of sensory appeal before expiration, such as chocolate bloom, clumping of dried fruit, staling, oxidation, or texture change from moisture, while the product remains safe. Model inputs may include time since stocking, lot data, temperature and humidity, handling patterns inferred from transactions, and appearance indicators derived from camera images. When the model predicts that full price will reduce sell-through, an accelerated discount schedule begins before expiration. (c) Space-yield displacement: the controller computes revenue per slot or per bay over a time window. If a product is still fresh but occupies a low-turnover slot, the price is lowered to clear the slot for higher-velocity stock.
[0111] In some embodiments, pricing is bidirectional: prices may rise as well as fall. A machine-learning model, a rule engine, or both consider inputs including expiration timestamps and time to expiry; real-time external market data, such as commodity values or competitor prices for the item category; demand and surge logic based on traffic, scarcity, or inventory velocity; local time of day and weather; and a location profile that sets base price tiers by deployment site, such as an airport versus a residential building, and local purchasing power.
[0112] In some embodiments, a shrinkage compensation engine adjusts unit pricing, for example exponentially, according to real-time moisture loss measured by the load cells. A space-yield displacement engine may set prices from a weighted aggregate of (i) optical and gravimetric sensory decay, correlating camera-based surface-texture analysis with moisture weight loss, (ii) spatial opportunity cost, the revenue potential of the volume occupied by a slow-moving item compared with a faster alternative, and (iii) contextual demand factors. The space-yield routines may also recommend restocking or relocating inventory among shelves, bins, and modules according to predicted demand, expiry, and temperature constraints, to maximize revenue per unit volume.
[0113] Pricing is constrained by safety rules and mechanical lockouts. For example, when the antenna matrix detects a tag whose expiration has passed, the controller disables dispensing of that item, including by mechanically disabling the associated pusher or gate, regardless of price.
O. Transaction settlement and multi-tenant operation
[0114] The transaction module 420 supports a real-time incremental authorization scheme for standalone and clustered deployments. In an instant-capture mode, funds are captured essentially immediately when a removal is confirmed. In an aggregated-capture mode, each confirmed removal (i) records a binding purchase commitment for the item in the local ledger or a cloud ledger and (ii) increments the authorization hold on the user's payment instrument, without capturing funds at the payment gateway 914 for each item. A single consolidated capture then occurs at session end. In both modes, access may be conditioned on a sufficient available authorization amount. The scheme preserves grab-and-go security while reducing per-item transaction fees.
[0115] In some embodiments the cabinet is a multi-tenant platform. Shelves, slots, or module zones are virtually allocated, for example leased, to different suppliers. The controller associates each stocked item or slot with a tenant identifier and keeps per-tenant inventory records. Through a supplier interface 904, each supplier can monitor inventory on its allocated shelves, receive alerts such as low stock or approaching purge, and update prices and promotions for its own products in real time, independently of other tenants and of the cabinet operator.
[0116] The transaction module splits a single user basket into per-tenant settlement legs and routes the funds for each removed item, or group of items, to the corresponding merchant account, such as the cabinet operator's account and one or more supplier accounts. In some embodiments the split is hardware-triggered: the shelf module identifier or zone sensor identifier that detected the removal is associated with a merchant account and determines the routing, automatically and without a user-selected "split bill" step. The user still sees a single experience and a consolidated receipt. The split may be executed as an instant split-payment smart contract that divides revenue between the platform operator and the supplier in real time, without a centralized holding period.
P. Clustered deployment
[0117] Referring to FIG. 15, the system may include several physical storage units coupled to a shared controller in a master-slave configuration. A master unit 850 houses higher-cost components, such as an AI processor, the payment terminal, the biometric camera, and fiscal printer logic. One or more adjacent slave units 852 provide additional storage and contain their own sensors and locks, which report to and are controlled by the master unit over a local bus 854. A slave unit may also carry an auxiliary display 856 for advertising, wayfinding, or navigation, even though payment and primary authentication are centralized at the master. The master maintains a single user session and a virtual basket across all units: a user authenticates at the master, retrieves items from several slave units, and receives a single consolidated capture and receipt. This architecture reduces the bill of materials per cabinet while allowing scalable installations at a site. Cabinets in a cluster may be adjacent, stacked, wall-integrated, or distributed and networked.

R. Application to open retail
[0121] Although many embodiments are described for a secure cabinet, the partitioned multi-tenant control architecture, the space-yield pricing engine, and the instant split-payment routing are not limited to enclosed systems. ABSTRACT
An autonomous retail cabinet has a chassis defining vertically spaced mounting positions and a position-adjustable interface, such as a busbar, drag chain, or flexible harness, that supplies power and data at each position. Interchangeable modules of different types, including pusher, hook, scale-box, gated gravity-feed, tray, auto-purge, thermal processing, and fluid processing modules, mount at any position. On docking, a controller reads each module's identifier and assigns that position a detection profile. Motorized leveling feet and a tilt sensor keep the cabinet level, and sessions are inhibited while it is out of level. Item removal by direct manual retrieval is confirmed by fusing internal camera output with the item-detection sensors named in the profile. A thermal processing module provides sealed induction heating with negative-pressure ventilation, pre-payment gating, a retrieval timer, and diversion of uncollected items to a cold waste compartment.

AUTONOMOUS RETAIL PLATFORM WITH VERIFIED FEEDBACK CONTROL   FIELD [0001] The present disclosure relates to autonomous retail systems, smart mini-store modules, distributed unattended retail networks, customer application interfaces, supplier interfaces, slot allocation engines, product reservation systems, remote order pickup systems, verified customer-feedback processing, quality-event handling, supplier restriction control, replenishment authorization, sale authorization, and platform-level control systems for governing products, suppliers, and workflows across multiple autonomous retail modules. BACKGROUND [0002] Autonomous retail cabinets, smart shelves, unattended mini-stores, and similar systems may allow consumers to purchase products without a conventional cashier or full-service store. Such systems may include cameras, shelf sensors, access control devices, payment systems, electronic displays, locks, and local controllers configured to detect item movement and complete transactions. [0003] Conventional autonomous retail systems generally focus on detecting what product was removed and charging a consumer for that product. However, many such systems do not provide a coordinated platform architecture that manages multiple suppliers, multiple product categories, multiple inventory ownership modes, multiple locations, and multiple autonomous retail modules under a common technical governance layer. [0004] A supplier may wish to place products directly into distributed autonomous modules, while a platform operator may control the module hardware, software, access rules, display behavior, payment logic, and settlement logic. A location owner may also receive a lease payment, service fee, or revenue share. These relationships can be difficult to implement technically because product availability, replenishment permission, slot assignment, sale authorization, and financial settlement must be synchronized across hardware and software systems. [0005] Existing systems may collect consumer ratings, complaints, or comments for general analytics. However, merely storing feedback or displaying ratings does not technically control 

[PAGE 2]
the autonomous retail infrastructure. A need exists for a platform in which verified customer-entered ratings, transaction-linked feedback, quality events, and system-generated quality indicators are converted into executable control actions that determine what products may be stocked, displayed, reserved, sold, replenished, released for pickup, or blocked within one or more autonomous retail modules. [0006] A need also exists for product reservation logic that allows a consumer to reserve an item already present in a selected mini-store while preserving at least one remaining unit for immediate walk-up purchase when appropriate. A further need exists for remote ordering logic in which a consumer can order a product for delivery into a selected autonomous mini-store, after which the product is held in an assigned slot or compartment and released only to the authorized consumer through the same platform identity and control logic. [0007] A further need exists for a hybrid autonomous retail platform that can operate in supplier-owned inventory mode, operator-owned retail mode, consignment mode, agency mode, or a combination of such modes, while using technical control loops to enforce supplier permissions, SKU allow-lists, replenishment restrictions, slot allocation, display states, reservation states, pickup entitlements, and sale enablement across a distributed network of autonomous retail modules. A. Modular Autonomous Retail Platform Architecture [0019] In some embodiments, a modular autonomous retail platform includes a plurality of autonomous retail modules, a platform controller, a consumer application, a supplier interface, a local module controller for each autonomous retail module, a supplier-to-slot assignment engine, a feedback processing engine, a quality-event engine, a dynamic pricing engine, a reservation engine, a remote-order pickup engine, and a split-payment engine. [0020] The autonomous retail modules may be deployed at residential buildings, office buildings, campuses, hotels, transportation hubs, gas station forecourts, malls, gyms, hospitals, apartment towers, neighborhood locations, or other convenience-oriented sites. Each module may include a cabinet, locker, refrigerated compartment, ambient compartment, vending enclosure, smart shelf, walk-in compact store, or other unattended product-holding structure. [0021] Each autonomous retail module may include one or more access doors, electronically controlled locks, shelf zones, product slots, bins, pickup compartments, cameras, depth sensors, load cells, RFID readers, NFC readers, barcode scanners, QR readers, environmental sensors, electronic shelf labels, displays, payment terminals, network interfaces, and a local edge controller. The local edge controller may maintain a local digital state of module contents and may communicate with the platform controller over a wired or wireless network. [0022] The platform controller may be cloud-hosted, locally hosted, regionally distributed, or divided between a central controller and one or more edge controllers. The platform controller may maintain consumer identities, supplier profiles, supplier admission status, SKU records, SKU allow-list status, module maps, slot registries, reservation records, pickup records, quality

Hybrid Platform and Inventory Ownership Modes [0023] In some embodiments, the platform operates as a hybrid mini-store system that combines a marketplace-style supplier access layer with managed retail infrastructure controlled by the platform operator. Suppliers may list or provide products directly for placement in the autonomous modules, while the platform operator controls the physical modules, access systems, sensing stack, payment integration, display behavior, software rules, and technical enforcement logic. [0024] The system may support multiple inventory ownership modes within the same platform network. In supplier-owned inventory mode, a supplier may retain title or commercial ownership of a product until sale and may receive proceeds after a consumer transaction. In operator-owned inventory mode, the platform operator may purchase, hold, or otherwise assume inventory risk and may control price and settlement. In consignment, agency, or hybrid modes, title, risk, pricing authority, quality responsibility, and settlement allocations may be determined by stored rules. [0025] The inventory ownership mode may affect slot assignment, pricing bounds, discount permissions, complaint reserves, settlement holdbacks, replenishment rights, return handling, spoilage handling, customer credits, supplier chargebacks, and sale authorization. The platform may store a mode value for each supplier, SKU, product batch, category, location, module, slot, or transaction and may enforce different technical workflows based on the stored mode value. C. Supplier Interface and Supplier Profiles [0026] The supplier interface may include a web portal, mobile application, API, dashboard, or combination thereof. Through the supplier interface, a supplier may submit product data, SKU identifiers, packaging data, temperature requirements, pricing constraints, restocking schedules, product images, regulatory data, category tags, batch records, and desired locations or slot types. [0027] A supplier profile may store supplier identity information, admission status, geographic eligibility, category permissions, quality history, complaint history, replenishment compliance, delivery performance, product recall data, insurance or certification status, settlement settings, slot access rights, and one or more supplier quality scores. The supplier profile may be updated 

[PAGE 6]
automatically based on verified transactions, customer ratings, quality events, operator inspections, failed deliveries, stock-out events, unauthorized replenishment attempts, or other platform signals. [0028] In some embodiments, a supplier profile includes one or more permission flags controlling whether the supplier may access the supplier interface, request slot placement, replenish a module, place a SKU in a module, view analytics, receive settlement, participate in reservation workflows, or supply products for remote-order pickup. The permission flags may be changed manually by an operator or automatically by the platform controller in response to feedback and quality-event metrics. D. Consumer Application and Verified Transaction Linkage [0029] The consumer application may provide account creation, identity verification, payment credential storage, module discovery, entry authorization, shopping session initiation, item scanning, reservation requests, remote-order placement, pickup confirmation, receipt access, customer support, product rating submission, complaint submission, and refund or credit requests. [0030] In preferred embodiments, customer feedback is linked to a verified transaction before it is eligible to affect supplier or product control decisions. A verified transaction may include a completed purchase, a recorded product removal event, a paid reservation, a completed pickup, a returned item, a refund request, a verified complaint, a quality rejection event, or another event tied to a consumer account and a specific product, SKU, supplier, batch, module, slot, or time window. [0031] The platform may reject, ignore, discount, quarantine, or separately store feedback that is not associated with a verified transaction. The platform may also weight feedback differently based on consumer history, product type, module location, time since purchase, quantity purchased, image evidence, operator confirmation, sensor evidence, refund status, or whether similar quality events occurred for other consumers or modules. E. Customer-Provided Numerical Quality Ratings [0032] In some embodiments, the consumer application receives a customer-provided numerical quality assessment for a purchased, reserved, picked-up, returned, or rejected product. The numerical quality assessment may be a star rating, numerical score, freshness score, taste score, 

[PAGE 7]
appearance score, packaging condition score, temperature score, satisfaction score, or other customer-entered value. [0033] The customer-provided numerical quality assessment may be associated with a specific SKU, supplier, batch, module, shelf zone, slot, location, pickup order, reservation, transaction, or product category. The platform may store the assessment together with metadata such as transaction identifier, consumer identifier, timestamp, module identifier, product identifier, batch identifier, product age, temperature history, image evidence, refund outcome, and complaint category. [0034] The platform may normalize customer-provided numerical ratings across products, categories, consumers, modules, or time periods. For example, the feedback processing engine may convert a star rating, freshness slider, complaint severity, refund request, return event, image-confirmed defect, or low-satisfaction score into a normalized quality-event value. The normalized value may be used to update a supplier quality score, SKU quality score, batch confidence score, location-specific trust score, or other derived metric. F. System-Generated Quality Indicators and Quality Events [0035] In some embodiments, the platform also receives system-generated quality indicators from module sensors, cameras, load cells, environmental sensors, time-based degradation models, inventory aging data, spoilage detection models, weight-loss measurements, temperature excursions, humidity readings, door-open duration, detected packaging damage, product rejection, product disposal, stock handling events, or operator inspection results. [0036] A quality event may include a low customer rating, repeated low ratings, a verified complaint, a refund request, an image-confirmed defect, a temperature excursion, an expired batch, a product recall, an abnormal removal pattern, a detected stock mismatch, repeated rejected products, customer disposal, operator quarantine, failed pickup quality inspection, or other event indicating that a product, SKU, supplier, batch, module, or location may require restriction or review. [0037] The quality-event engine may combine customer-provided numerical ratings with system-generated quality indicators to compute derived metrics. The derived metrics may include a supplier quality score, SKU quality score, batch confidence score, module trust score, location-

specific supplier score, product availability score, pickup release confidence value, replenishment confidence value, or sale authorization score. G. Feedback-to-Control Conversion [0038] In some embodiments, the feedback processing engine does not merely generate analytics or reports. Instead, the engine converts verified customer-entered ratings, transaction-linked feedback, complaints, rejection events, and quality events into executable technical control outputs that are enforced by the platform controller and one or more local module controllers. [0039] Executable technical control outputs may include adding a SKU to an allow-list, removing a SKU from an allow-list, changing a supplier admission status, authorizing replenishment, denying replenishment, reducing slot allocation priority, expanding slot allocation priority, blocking sale authorization, enabling sale authorization, preventing reservation of affected products, allowing reservation of affected products, suspending pickup release, enabling pickup release, changing a displayed product state, initiating inspection, commanding a purge, locking a door, disabling a slot, or sending an access command to a local controller. [0040] The platform may apply threshold rules, rolling averages, decay functions, anomaly detection, confidence intervals, Bayesian updating, machine-learning models, complaint clustering, batch correlation, module comparison, or cross-location trend analysis to determine whether a feedback or quality-event metric should trigger a control action. The platform may require one event for severe quality conditions or multiple events for lower-confidence signals. [0041] In some embodiments, the platform maintains a rule table mapping particular feedback states or quality-event states to particular hardware or software control outputs. For example, if a SKU quality score falls below a threshold, the platform may disable sale authorization for the SKU in affected modules, hide or change the corresponding shelf display, prevent new reservations, block remote-order pickup release, and notify the supplier or operator. H. Supplier-to-Slot Assignment Engine [0042] The supplier-to-slot assignment engine determines which supplier or SKU should occupy a shelf zone, slot, bin, compartment, display area, temperature zone, pickup compartment, or other module location. The assignment may be based on demand forecasts, category fit, turnover, price elasticity, available space, slot yield, local consumer behavior, product quality, supplier 

[PAGE 9]
quality score, complaint frequency, replenishment reliability, distance to module, margin, expiration risk, reservation demand, pickup demand, or platform rules. [0043] The supplier-to-slot assignment engine may generate slot permissions rather than merely recommendations. For example, the engine may grant a supplier access to a particular slot, deny access to a slot, reserve a slot for a higher-performing supplier, reduce slot quantity assigned to a low-scoring SKU, or reassign a slot after repeated quality events. The local module controller may enforce the assignment by enabling or disabling access doors, replenishment compartments, electronic labels, slot lights, product database states, or sale authorization states. [0044] In some embodiments, slot allocation is dynamic and may be recalculated periodically or in response to trigger events. Trigger events may include a change in demand, a low quality score, a high quality score, a verified complaint, a recall, a restocking failure, a location-specific trend, a reservation spike, a pickup-order backlog, weather, seasonality, expiration risk, or operator input. I. SKU Allow-Listing and Sale Authorization [0045] The platform may maintain one or more SKU allow-lists for each supplier, module, location, category, temperature zone, shelf zone, or slot. A SKU allow-list may define whether a product is eligible to be stocked, displayed, reserved, sold, replenished, transferred, purged, discounted, or delivered into a selected module. [0046] If feedback or quality-event metrics indicate a problem with a SKU, the platform may remove the SKU from an allow-list, place the SKU into a restricted state, require operator approval before replenishment, limit the SKU to certain locations, block future slot assignments, or require additional inspection before sale. If metrics improve, the platform may restore the SKU to an allow-list or expand permitted slot allocation. [0047] Sale authorization may be controlled at the product, SKU, batch, supplier, slot, module, location, or network level. When sale authorization is disabled, the platform may prevent checkout, remove the item from the customer application, disable electronic shelf labels, block product selection, lock a compartment, generate a warning display, mark the product for operator pickup, or initiate a purge or quarantine workflow. J. Replenishment Authorization and Supplier Access Control 

[PAGE 10]
[0048] The platform may control replenishment authorization for suppliers, couriers, operators, or automated restocking systems. Replenishment authorization may define who may open a module, which door or compartment may be opened, which SKU may be inserted, how many units may be added, what batch identifier must be scanned, what temperature or packaging condition must be confirmed, and whether the replenishment event is accepted into inventory. [0049] A supplier or courier may request replenishment through the supplier interface, a courier application, a service credential, an NFC badge, a QR code, a barcode, biometric authentication, or an API. The platform may approve or deny the request based on supplier status, SKU allow-list status, slot assignment, quality score, batch confidence, time window, capacity, access history, recall status, or module state. [0050] If a supplier quality score falls below a threshold, a SKU receives repeated verified low ratings, or a quality event is confirmed, the platform may deny replenishment access, restrict replenishment quantity, require inspection, redirect replenishment to another module, disable supplier credentials, or require operator approval before any additional product is stocked. The local module controller may enforce the decision by refusing to unlock a door or compartment. K. Product Reservation Module [0051] In some embodiments, the reservation module allows a consumer to reserve one or more specific products that are already physically present in a selected autonomous retail module. The consumer may use the consumer application to select a module, view available products, request a reservation, and receive a reservation window associated with the consumer account. [0052] Before confirming a reservation, the platform may check real-time inventory state, SKU allow-list status, sale authorization state, supplier status, quality score, batch confidence score, product age, module availability, slot state, and whether a last-unit protection rule applies. The platform may then mark a reserved quantity in the module inventory record and synchronize the reserved state to the local module controller. [0053] In some embodiments, the platform applies a last-unit protection rule. If only one unit of a requested product remains in the selected module, that final unit is not reservable remotely and remains available for immediate in-person purchase. In other embodiments, a configurable number of units greater than one may be protected for walk-up consumers, or the protection rule 

[PAGE 11]
may vary by product type, location, time of day, consumer status, supplier rules, or operator rules. [0054] Reservation eligibility may also be controlled by verified customer feedback and quality-event metrics. For example, if a SKU or batch has a low quality score, unresolved complaint cluster, temperature excursion, or sale authorization hold, the reservation module may prevent reservation, show a warning, require consumer acknowledgement, apply a discount, or route the product to inspection. L. Remote Order with Pickup Storage [0055] In some embodiments, the remote-order pickup module allows a consumer to order a product through the consumer application even when the product is not yet physically present in the selected autonomous retail module. The platform may select a fulfillment source, generate a replenishment or transfer task, assign a destination module, assign a slot or pickup compartment, and create a pickup entitlement linked to the consumer account. [0056] When the ordered product is delivered into the destination module, the local module controller may verify the product identifier, supplier, batch identifier, temperature status, delivery credential, slot assignment, and pickup entitlement. The product may then be stored in an assigned compartment, shelf zone, locker, refrigerated compartment, or pickup bin until the pickup window expires or the consumer retrieves the product. [0057] Pickup release may be controlled by the platform controller and the local module controller. The platform may release the assigned compartment or otherwise authorize product removal only after verifying consumer identity, pickup entitlement, payment status, product status, quality status, reservation state, and module state. If a quality event occurs after delivery but before pickup, the platform may suspend release, issue a replacement, request inspection, cancel the order, refund the consumer, or reassign another product. [0058] Remote-order pickup logic may be integrated with supplier restriction and quality-control rules. For example, a supplier with a restricted status may be prevented from delivering products into modules for pickup; a SKU removed from an allow-list may not be accepted into a pickup compartment; and a low batch confidence score may cause pickup release to be blocked until inspection is completed. M. Dynamic Pricing, Displays, and Availability States 

[PAGE 12]
[0059] The dynamic pricing engine may adjust product price based on supplier-defined bounds, operator-defined rules, demand, stock age, product quality, customer ratings, complaint history, module location, time of day, weather, seasonality, stock level, reservation demand, pickup demand, remaining shelf life, opportunity cost of a slot, or a quality-event state. [0060] The platform may control electronic shelf labels, module displays, customer application displays, pickup status displays, product availability indicators, and warning indicators. Display states may include available, reserved, pickup-only, blocked, under inspection, discounted, quality warning, last unit not reservable, supplier restricted, sale disabled, replenishment denied, or operator review required. [0061] In some embodiments, a display change is itself a technical control output caused by verified feedback or quality events. For example, repeated low customer ratings for a batch may cause the platform to change the shelf display to a clearance price, hide the product from the reservation interface, show a quality warning, or disable a purchase button in the consumer application. [0062] In some embodiments, the autonomous retail module enforces a sales-final or non-returnable transaction workflow for goods removed from a shelf, cabinet, drawer, slot, bin, tray, hook, pusher, compartment, or other product-holding region. Before a user session is initiated, before an access barrier is unlocked, or before a product-holding region is enabled for product removal, the platform may present transaction terms through a cabinet-door monitor, door-integrated display, shelf-edge display, payment-terminal display, companion mobile application, or other user interface. The transaction terms may state that removal of a product constitutes acceptance of purchase, that the removed product will be charged immediately or committed to a real-time incremental authorization or hold, and that the product is non-returnable except under defined exceptions such as verified defect, spoilage, recall, regulatory requirement, or operator-approved refund. [0063] The platform may require an affirmative consent input before enabling access to the product-holding region. The affirmative consent input may include selecting an “I Agree” control, tapping an accept button on a cabinet-door monitor, confirming through the consumer application, selecting a checkbox, scanning a session token, presenting a biometric credential, or otherwise providing an electronic acknowledgement. The platform may store a time-stamped 

[PAGE 13]
consent record in a local ledger, cloud ledger, audit log, or transaction record and may associate the consent record with the user session, payment instrument, access event, and one or more product-removal events. [0064] After the affirmative consent input is received, the platform may unlock the access barrier or otherwise enable product access. When a product removal is detected by one or more sensors, including a camera, RFID reader, NFC reader, antenna matrix, optical beam sensor, load cell, shelf sensor, drawer sensor, door sensor, or local module controller, the platform may automatically identify the removed product, update inventory, generate a transaction record, charge a stored payment credential, capture funds from a pre-authorization, increment a reserved amount, or record a legally binding purchase commitment for the removed product. The charge or commitment may occur substantially in real time upon detection of removal or may be captured as part of an aggregated transaction at session termination. [0065] In some embodiments, the module further includes anti-return or anti-reverse enforcement. Mechanical anti-reverse elements, such as ratchets, pawls, stops, directional teeth, one-way pushers, hook stops, or gated gravity-feed mechanisms, may physically inhibit reinsertion or backward movement of a product after the product has advanced past a removal threshold. For weight-based or open product zones, the controller may detect an attempted return based on a weight increase, RFID reappearance, optical-beam event, camera-observed return gesture, drawer event, or other sensor-fusion output during an active session. In response to an unauthorized return attempt, the platform may maintain the original charge, apply a return penalty, apply a second charge, apply a punitive charge based on a stored rule, flag the session for review, restrict future access, or generate an audit record. N. Split-Payment and Settlement Engine [0066] The split-payment engine may allocate transaction proceeds among a supplier, platform operator, location owner, delivery provider, payment processor, service provider, or other authorized participant. Allocation rules may depend on inventory ownership mode, supplier agreement, location agreement, category, slot type, reservation state, pickup state, promotion source, complaint reserve, refund reserve, spoilage reserve, holdback amount, quality score, or supplier restriction status. 

[PAGE 14]
[0067] In supplier-owned inventory mode, the system may direct a supplier share to the supplier, an operator fee to the platform operator, and a lease or revenue-share amount to the location owner. In operator-owned inventory mode, the platform operator may receive product revenue while paying the location owner and other participants according to stored rules. In consignment or hybrid modes, rules may allocate revenue based on title transfer, sale price, discounts, service fees, credits, or chargebacks. [0068] Quality events and customer ratings may affect settlement. For example, a low batch confidence score, verified complaint, refund event, or quality hold may cause the platform to withhold supplier proceeds, create a reserve, issue a customer credit, charge back a supplier, reduce a supplier payout, suspend settlement, or route the transaction to manual review. These financial outputs may be linked to the same technical control loop that restricts sale, replenishment, reservation, or pickup workflows. O. Network-Level Synchronization and Local Enforcement [0069] The platform may synchronize restrictions, permissions, and state changes with local module controllers. A restriction generated at the platform level may be transmitted to affected modules as a command, policy update, configuration update, lockout rule, SKU state update, display update, reservation state update, pickup entitlement update, or sale authorization update. [0070] Each local module controller may enforce received control outputs even when network communication is degraded. For example, a module may store a local cache of blocked SKUs, restricted suppliers, disabled slots, pending pickup entitlements, last-unit reservation rules, or quality holds. The local module controller may continue to block sale or pickup of restricted products until a valid platform update is received. [0071] The system may maintain audit logs of feedback inputs, quality-event inputs, derived metrics, control decisions, command transmissions, local enforcement actions, override decisions, and settlement effects. These logs may allow later review of why a supplier, SKU, slot, reservation, pickup order, or sale was permitted, denied, blocked, released, discounted, or held. P. Example Operation [0072] In an example operation, a supplier requests placement of refrigerated beverages in several autonomous retail modules through the supplier interface. The platform evaluates the 

[PAGE 15]
supplier profile, SKU allow-list status, category rules, available refrigerated slots, location demand, and quality score. The supplier-to-slot assignment engine grants the supplier access to two refrigerated slots in a selected module and transmits the slot permission to the corresponding local module controller. [0073] After products are stocked, consumers purchase products through autonomous shopping sessions. The consumer application invites verified purchasers to enter numerical quality ratings. Several verified consumers submit low quality ratings and complaints associated with one batch. A camera or temperature sensor also records a possible quality event. The feedback processing engine normalizes the inputs and lowers a batch confidence score and SKU quality score. [0074] Because the batch confidence score falls below a threshold, the platform controller transmits commands to affected modules to block additional sale authorization for the batch, change the shelf display to under inspection, prevent new reservations, suspend pickup release for pending remote orders containing the batch, deny replenishment of the SKU by the supplier, and notify an operator. The split-payment engine may also hold settlement proceeds for transactions associated with the affected batch pending review. [0075] In another example, a consumer attempts to reserve the last remaining unit of a product through the consumer application. The reservation module checks the inventory state and determines that only one unit remains. The platform denies the reservation under a last-unit protection rule while continuing to allow an in-person customer at the module to purchase the final unit if the SKU remains sale-authorized. [0076] In another example, a consumer remotely orders a product for pickup at a selected module. The platform assigns a pickup compartment, generates a delivery task, records a pickup entitlement, and instructs the local module controller to accept the product only if the SKU, supplier, and batch are authorized. After delivery, the compartment is held for the consumer. If a quality event occurs before pickup, the platform may suspend release until inspection or replacement occurs. Q.

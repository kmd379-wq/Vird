SMART RETAIL CABINET WITH TOKENIZED ACCESS AND SPLIT PAYMENTS  Field of the Invention The present disclosure relates to autonomous retail cabinets and networked vending systems and, more particularly, to modular smart cabinets having tokenized user access, reconfigurable shelf architectures with position‑adjustable power/data coupling, multi‑tenant inventory management, split‑routed payment processing, and resilient offline operation supported by local ledgering and backup power. Background Conventional vending machines and “grab‑and‑go” cabinets typically provide limited flexibility for rapidly reconfiguring shelf layouts, and may require manual rewiring or service intervention when shelf positions are changed. Such systems can also be vulnerable to fraud and chargebacks where transaction terms are not clearly presented to users prior to access, and may become inoperative or insecure during network outages or external power loss. Further, existing solutions often lack robust sensor architectures that simultaneously verify user identity and accurately track item removals and returns, particularly for restricted goods requiring age verification and auditability. Additionally, modern retail deployments increasingly involve multi‑tenant inventory models in which different suppliers control pricing and inventory for distinct cabinet regions, and business models that require split routing of settlement funds to multiple merchant accounts. Operators also seek scalable multi‑cabinet installations (e.g., clustered or master‑slave configurations) that share costly components while maintaining a unified user session and consolidated settlement, including fee‑optimized payment capture across multiple item removal events. Accordingly, there remains a need for improved modular cabinet architectures, user‑consent workflows, resilient transaction processing, and secure multi‑tenant settlement. Detailed Description In one embodiment, and with reference to FIG. 1, a universal smart cabinet system is provided for autonomous retail of goods, including perishable items and restricted items. The system comprises at least one enclosure defining a storage volume and at least one access barrier (e.g., a door) configured to be selectively locked and unlocked under control of a cabinet controller. The enclosure supports a plurality of interchangeable merchandising modules that may be rearranged without tool‑intensive rework to accommodate different product form factors. In certain embodiments, the cabinet operates as a node of a networked platform and/or as part of a clustered installation comprising multiple enclosures that share one or more processing or transaction resources, as described further below. I. Hardware Chassis (Transformer Base) In embodiments, the unified chassis includes a position‑adjustable interface extending along a rear wall or other structural member of the cabinet, the interface being configured to distribute electrical power and/or data to one or more removable or repositionable shelves while permitting vertical repositioning of a given shelf between multiple discrete or continuous height positions without requiring disconnection and re-termination of wiring. The position‑adjustable interface may further support hot‑swap identification of a shelf module and bidirectional communication between the shelf module and an edge controller. In non‑limiting implementations, the position‑adjustable interface is selected from the group consisting of: (A) a conductive busbar or rail system providing sliding contacts and/or inductive couplers that couple to a shelf module when docked at a selected vertical position; (B) a drag‑chain (cable carrier) that guides one or more conductors between the edge controller and a shelf module as the shelf module is repositioned; and (C) a flexible wire harness defining an umbilical or service loop with sufficient slack to allow vertical repositioning of the shelf module

while maintaining electrical continuity. In each case, the interface enables rapid vertical reconfiguration while maintaining a defined power/data coupling between the shelf module and the cabinet electronics. In embodiments, the cabinet further includes a mobility system comprising industrial casters for rolling movement and retractable leveling feet for stabilization. The mobility system enables a service operator to reposition the cabinet for maintenance, cleaning, and rear loading, while the leveling feet provide a stable and repeatable reference plane for weight‑based sensing and dispensing operations. In embodiments, each module includes a smart edge along a user‑facing front portion of a shelf or module. The smart edge may include a high‑density antenna matrix (i.e., an array of antennas rather than a single antenna) and an LED information strip. The antenna matrix and LED strip cooperate to (i) detect tagged items within defined pick zones, (ii) indicate item locations and dynamic prices, and (iii) provide user navigation and status feedback during a shopping session. With reference to FIG. 2, the system further includes a processing subsystem executed by an edge controller and/or remote computing resources, the processing subsystem being configured to determine, for a cabinet session, which physical items are removed from and/or returned to the cabinet and to associate such item events with a specific user and payment instrument. In embodiments, the processing subsystem receives sensor outputs from a sensor suite that includes (i) an external biometric imaging zone mounted on an exterior chassis surface, door bezel, or adjacent kiosk structure, the external biometric imaging zone being configured to capture facial features or other biometric traits used for identity verification and/or age verification, and (ii) an internal inventory imaging zone disposed within a storage volume of the cabinet (e.g., overhead and/or side‑mounted cameras) configured to observe shelf regions, product movement, and user hand interactions. The sensor suite may further include a high‑density antenna matrix disposed along shelf fronts, one or more load cells, and optional auxiliary sensors (e.g., door position sensors, proximity sensors, and/or lighting sensors). In one example, restricted‑goods transactions are enabled only upon corroboration by a fusion routine that combines outputs of the internal inventory imaging zone, the antenna matrix, and the load cells, thereby reducing the risk of tag swapping and enabling detection of partial‑consumption behaviors (e.g., opening a bottle and returning it) that may not be reliably detected by RFID alone. The processing subsystem may generate a transaction record that includes identified items, quantities, timestamps, and audit artifacts and may route funds and digital receipts as between a cabinet operator account and one or more third‑party supplier accounts, as described further below. II. Module Variety (Interchangeable) The cabinet may be populated with a plurality of different merchandising modules, including, by way of example: Module A (pushers including springs and dividers for rigid packages), Module B (hooks or rods for hanging items), Module C (box‑on‑scale modules for bulk items), Module D (gated gravity feed modules), Module F (sliding trays with low‑friction floors for soft packaging), and Module G (auto‑purge modules). Modules may be inserted, removed, and vertically repositioned relative to the transformer base, and may be simultaneously present in a 

[PAGE 4]

single cabinet. Each module may include local sensing and/or actuation (e.g., gates, latches, motors, load cells) that report to the cabinet controller via the vertical backbone. Module D (Gated Gravity Feed). In one embodiment, a gated gravity feed module comprises an inclined chute terminating at a locking gate and a weighing dish positioned to receive a dispensed item. The cabinet controller is configured to open the gate to dispense one item from the chute to the weighing dish, and to lock the gate while the weighing dish detects non‑zero weight. The gate remains locked until the user physically removes the item such that the weight returns to zero, thereby preventing sweeping of multiple items and enabling precise, single‑item accounting. III. Induction and Inventory Manifesting.  The system supports an “induction” process for associating physical goods with digital inventory records, including tracking tag identifiers (e.g., RFID UID), SKU identifiers, batch identifiers, and expiration data. In certain embodiments, induction may be performed locally at or near the cabinet using an induction station that reads tags and records associated product attributes. In addition or alternatively, induction may be performed remotely at an upstream supplier warehouse or distribution center, where tags are applied to goods and the tag‑to‑product association is created off‑site. In such upstream embodiments, a supplier system uploads an allow‑list or inventory manifest to a remote server, and the cabinet controller downloads the manifest prior to or upon receipt of goods so that the cabinet can recognize the pre‑tagged goods without requiring local scanning of each item. In embodiments, the cabinet controller maintains inventory state by comparing observed tag reads and/or sensor‑fusion outputs to the allow‑list manifest. When a pre‑tagged item arrives at the cabinet, the cabinet recognizes the item based on the uploaded association and transitions the item into an “active” inventory state without requiring a local induction step. Local induction may still be used for exceptions, returns to stock, or ad hoc stocking by an operator. IV. Secure Unit “A” and Dual‑Mode Operation In embodiments, a portion of the cabinet defines a secure compartment for restricted goods (e.g., tobacco or alcohol). The secure compartment may include switchable smart glass (e.g., PDLC) that is opaque in a sleep mode and transitions to transparent in an inspection mode after successful verification. Verification may include biometric verification and/or token‑based verification using a QR code from a KYC‑verified account. In embodiments, the controller enforces a triple‑sensor fusion requirement (antenna matrix + overhead imaging + load cells) for restricted goods, including to prevent partial consumption or tag swapping. A single cabinet or compartment may dynamically switch between a standard mode and a restricted mode based on detected product type, a time‑of‑day rule (e.g., automatic lockout after a specified time), and/or a remote command, without changing physical hardware. V. “No Return” Policy (Anti‑Reverse System) 

[PAGE 5]
 5 
In embodiments, the system enforces a “sales final” policy using one or more anti‑reverse mechanisms. In a first implementation, one‑way mechanical elements (e.g., ratchets, stops, or pawls) are integrated into pushers, hooks, and/or gated gravity‑feed mechanisms such that backward movement that would enable reinsertion is physically inhibited. In a second implementation, for shelf zones that do not employ one‑way mechanical elements (e.g., bins or sliding trays), the edge controller executes return‑detection logic using load‑cell data and/or inventory imaging, and may apply a software penalty (e.g., a second charge or other fee) when a weight increase indicates an attempted return after removal. To reduce chargeback risk and to provide an auditable user assent, the user interface presents, prior to unlocking a door or otherwise enabling access, the applicable transaction terms for the requested session, including (by way of example) an immediate‑charge or incremental‑hold policy, a no‑returns policy, and any penalty logic for detected returns. The user interface requires an affirmative user input (e.g., selection of an “I Agree” control) before an unlock signal is issued. The system may store a time‑stamped consent record in a local and/or cloud ledger and associate the consent record with the session identifier and transaction record. VI. Auto‑Purge and Smart Waste In embodiments, the cabinet automatically clears expired or designated items to free shelf space. A purge mechanism may include a retractable front stop that drops, allowing a spring to push an item forward until it falls into a bottom compartment. The bottom compartment may include load cells and RFID sensing to track purged items as “quarantined” pending collection. In embodiments, an anti‑scavenger rule may charge a defined value (e.g., original price) if a user retrieves an item from the waste compartment. VII. User Identification, Receipting, and Offline Operation Prior to granting access, the cabinet controller may require user identification via a biometric sensor and/or scanning of an optical code generated by a mobile application. The optical code may encode or reference a session token that links to a user profile and one or more payment instruments. The transaction processing module may reserve funds via pre‑authorization based on the session token, and may verify that the identity associated with the selected payment instrument matches the registered user profile (e.g., via KYC and/or issuer verification) to reduce fraud. In embodiments, scanning the optical code from a KYC‑verified account constitutes sufficient age verification for restricted goods, thereby allowing restricted‑mode access without mandatory camera capture in certain jurisdictions or configurations. In embodiments, upon transaction capture (e.g., at the time an item removal is finalized), the transaction processing module generates a digital receipt and, where required by local tax rules, a fiscal receipt, and transmits the receipt to the user’s mobile application and/or registered email address. The cabinet user interface may present pre‑payment information including exact expiration dates, scheduled purge times, and real‑time price changes for the specific items available. In embodiments, the transaction processing module applies a real‑time incremental hold such that funds are blocked on the payment instrument in near‑real time per item removed, rather than aggregated only at session end.

In embodiments, the cabinet controller maintains a robust offline financial ledger and an offline deposit buffer. If network connectivity is lost, the controller continues to authorize access based on locally stored session tokens and/or a locally stored balance, debits funds against the local ledger, and stores transaction history for asynchronous reconciliation once connectivity is restored. Accordingly, the cabinet remains operative for sales during network outages while preserving auditability and eventual settlement integrity. In embodiments, the cabinet hardware includes an integrated uninterruptible power supply (UPS) and/or battery unit mounted within the chassis and electrically coupled to at least the electronic locks, door sensors, and the cabinet controller. The UPS/battery unit is configured to maintain a secure state of the cabinet and to enable transaction continuity during external power loss, including continued acceptance of tokenized credentials, continued operation of sensors needed to detect removals, and continued recording of transaction events into the local financial ledger for asynchronous reconciliation. The UPS/battery unit may further provide controlled shutdown behavior and tamper‑event logging when backup power falls below a threshold. VIII. Pricing and Remote Controls The pricing module may implement time‑based decay, sensory decay based on detected quality loss, and space‑yield discounts based on slot stagnation. Pricing rules may apply across goods categories including food and non‑food items. In embodiments, pricing rules and thresholds may be updated remotely, including by an operator console and/or by supplier consoles associated with multi‑tenant shelf rental, as described below. IX. Multi‑Tenant Shelf Rental and Split Payments In embodiments, the cabinet is configured as a multi‑tenant platform in which defined shelves, slots, or module zones are virtually allocated (e.g., leased) to different suppliers. The controller associates each stocked item and/or slot with a tenant identifier and maintains per‑tenant inventory visibility and controls. A remote supplier interface enables a supplier to monitor inventory on its allocated shelves, receive alerts (e.g., low stock, approaching purge), and dynamically update prices and promotions for its own products in real time, independent of other tenants. In embodiments, the transaction processing module performs basket splitting such that a single user session is divided into per‑tenant settlement legs, routing funds for each removed item (or item group) to a corresponding merchant account (e.g., cabinet operator account and/or supplier account), while presenting a unified user experience and consolidated receipt. X. Clustered Deployment (Master‑Slave) In embodiments, the system comprises a plurality of physical storage units coupled to a shared controller in a master‑slave configuration. A master unit may house higher‑cost components such as an AI processor, payment terminal, biometric scanner, and/or fiscal printer logic, while one or more adjacent slave units provide additional storage volume and include sensors and locks that report to, and are controlled by, the master unit. The shared controller maintains a single user session and a “virtual basket” across multiple enclosures such that a user may authenticate (e.g., by scanning a QR code) at the master unit, retrieve items from multiple slave units, and receive a 

[PAGE 7]
 7 
single consolidated fiscal receipt and payment capture for the session. This architecture reduces per‑cabinet bill‑of‑materials cost while enabling scalable multi‑unit installations at a single location. In certain embodiments, transaction settlement is performed according to a real‑time incremental authorization scheme that supports both standalone and clustered deployments while reducing per‑item transaction fees. In a first mode (Instant Capture), the system captures funds substantially immediately upon confirmation of an item‑removal event for a given cabinet session. In a second mode (Aggregated Capture), upon each confirmed item‑removal event the system (i) records a legally binding purchase commitment for the item in a local and/or cloud ledger and (ii) increments an authorization hold or pre‑authorization amount associated with the user’s payment instrument, without necessarily capturing funds at the payment gateway for each individual item. The system then triggers a consolidated capture at session termination (e.g., upon door closure, user checkout action, timeout, or departure from a cluster) to settle a single aggregated transaction reflecting items removed from one or more cabinets in the cluster. In both modes, access may be conditioned on a sufficient available authorization amount, thereby preserving grab‑and‑go security while enabling fee‑optimized settlement.

Abstract A retail cabinet includes an enclosure with an access barrier and a reconfigurable chassis supporting merchandising modules including pushers, hooks, box‑on‑scale modules, sliding trays, and gated gravity feeds configured to release a next item only after removal of a prior item. Repositionable shelves couple to a controller via an adjustable power/data interface implemented using a conductive busbar with sliding contacts, a drag‑chain cable carrier, or a flexible wire harness. A user session is established via biometric input and/or an optical code token from a mobile application, and a user interface presents transaction terms and requires affirmative consent prior to unlocking. Item removals are determined by sensor fusion using imaging, RFID antenna matrices, and load cells. Transactions may be settled by immediate capture or by incremental authorization holds with consolidated capture, including split‑routing to multiple payees, with an offline ledger and a UPS/battery enabling secure operation during network or power interruptions.

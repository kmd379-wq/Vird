SMART RETAIL CABINET WITH TOKENIZED ACCESS AND SPLIT PAYMENTS   Field of the Invention The present disclosure relates to autonomous retail cabinets and networked vending systems and, more particularly, to modular smart cabinets having tokenized user access, reconfigurable shelf architectures with position‑adjustable power/data coupling, multi‑tenant inventory management, split‑routed payment processing, and resilient offline operation supported by local ledgering and backup power. Background Conventional vending machines and “grab‑and‑go” cabinets typically provide limited flexibility for rapidly reconfiguring shelf layouts, and may require manual rewiring or service intervention when shelf positions are changed. Such systems can also be vulnerable to fraud and chargebacks where transaction terms are not clearly presented to users prior to access, and may become inoperative or insecure during network outages or external power loss. Further, existing solutions often lack robust sensor architectures that simultaneously verify user identity and accurately track item removals and returns, particularly for restricted goods requiring age verification and auditability. Additionally, modern retail deployments increasingly involve multi‑tenant inventory models in which different suppliers control pricing and inventory for distinct cabinet regions, and business models that require split routing of settlement funds to multiple merchant accounts. Operators also seek scalable multi‑cabinet installations (e.g., clustered or master‑slave configurations) that share costly components while maintaining a unified user session and consolidated settlement, including fee‑optimized payment capture across multiple item removal events. Accordingly, there remains a need for improved modular cabinet architectures, user‑consent workflows, resilient transaction processing, and secure multi‑tenant settlement. Detailed Description In one embodiment, and with reference to FIG. 1, a universal smart cabinet system is provided for autonomous retail of goods, including perishable items and restricted items. The system comprises at least one enclosure defining a storage volume and at least one access barrier (e.g., a door) configured to be selectively locked and unlocked under control of a cabinet controller. The enclosure supports a plurality of interchangeable merchandising modules that may be rearranged without tool‑intensive rework to accommodate different product form factors. In certain embodiments, the cabinet operates as a node of a networked platform and/or as part of a clustered installation comprising multiple enclosures that share one or more processing or transaction resources, as described further below. I. Hardware Chassis (Transformer Base) In embodiments, the unified chassis includes a position‑adjustable interface extending along a rear wall or other structural member of the cabinet, the interface being configured to distribute electrical power and/or data to one or more removable or repositionable shelves while permitting vertical repositioning of a given shelf between multiple discrete or continuous height positions without requiring disconnection and re-termination of wiring. The position‑adjustable interface may further support hot‑swap identification of a shelf module and bidirectional communication between the shelf module and an edge controller. In non‑limiting implementations, the position‑adjustable interface is selected from the group consisting of: (A) a conductive busbar or rail system providing sliding contacts and/or inductive couplers that couple to a shelf module when docked at a selected vertical position; (B) a drag‑chain (cable carrier) that guides one or more conductors between the edge controller and a 

[PAGE 3]
shelf module as the shelf module is repositioned; and (C) a flexible wire harness defining an umbilical or service loop with sufficient slack to allow vertical repositioning of the shelf module while maintaining electrical continuity. In each case, the interface enables rapid vertical reconfiguration while maintaining a defined power/data coupling between the shelf module and the cabinet electronics. In embodiments, the cabinet further includes a mobility system comprising industrial casters for rolling movement and an active motorized leveling system to ensure weighing accuracy and to reduce fraud. The chassis comprises a plurality of motorized leveling feet having electric actuators (e.g., screw-drive actuators) coupled to the cabinet controller. An integrated inclinometer/tilt sensor is mounted to the chassis frame and continuously outputs a tilt angle. If the detected tilt exceeds a calibration threshold (e.g., 0.5 degrees from true horizon), the controller automatically drives the actuators to level the cabinet frame and may inhibit initiation of a user session and/or transaction until leveling is within the threshold. The motorized leveling may be performed at startup, periodically, and/or upon detection of abnormal movement or vibration to maintain a stable reference plane for load‑cell sensing and dispensing operations. In embodiments, each module includes a smart edge along a user‑facing front portion of a shelf or module. The smart edge may include a high‑density antenna matrix (i.e., an array of antennas rather than a single antenna) and a shelf‑edge information system selected from an electronic shelf label (ESL), an LCD strip, or other electronic display. The shelf‑edge information system is configured to display alphanumeric data including real‑time dynamic pricing (changeable based on rules) and variable batch metadata (e.g., lot/batch identifiers and expiration dates) corresponding to the specific item at the front of a physical or virtual queue. In one embodiment, the controller maintains a virtual FIFO queue of expiration timestamps corresponding to the physical loading sequence for a given shelf zone, and upon detection of an item removal the controller automatically updates the shelf‑edge display to show the metadata (e.g., next expiration date and current price) for the next item in the queue. With reference to FIG. 2, the system further includes a processing subsystem executed by an edge controller and/or remote computing resources, the processing subsystem being configured to determine, for a cabinet session, which physical items are removed from and/or returned to the cabinet and to associate such item events with a specific user and payment instrument. In embodiments, internal cameras are always present and define a continuous visual tracking layer (the “Visual Tracking Layer”) disposed within the storage volume (e.g., overhead and/or side‑mounted cameras) and configured to track user hand movement, item interaction, and product movement within shelf regions. The sensor suite further includes an external user‑identification imaging sensor disposed on an exterior chassis surface, door bezel, or adjacent kiosk structure and configured to capture facial features or other biometric traits for identity verification and/or age verification.  In addition to the Visual Tracking Layer, the system employs a selectable physical detection 

[PAGE 4]
layer for specific shelf zones. In a first configuration for high‑value zones, the physical detection layer includes load cells (weight sensors) and RFID sensing (e.g., antenna matrix reads) and optionally other sensors. In a second configuration for standard zones, the physical detection layer includes optical beam sensors (e.g., break‑beam sensors) and mechanical anti‑reverse inhibitors integrated into associated pushers and/or hooks, without requiring load cells. The controller fuses outputs of the Visual Tracking Layer with outputs of the selected physical detection layer to validate item removals, detect unauthorized returns, and generate auditable transaction records, thereby reducing chargebacks and fraud. In embodiments, the chassis frame provides dual access architecture including front access for users via the primary access barrier and rear loading for operators via a service door or rear access panel. The rear loading configuration supports pass‑through installations (e.g., wall‑mounted or walk‑in cooler deployments) and facilitates physical FIFO stocking (e.g., loading from the rear while users retrieve from the front). II. Module Variety (Interchangeable) The cabinet may be populated with a plurality of different merchandising modules, including, by way of example: Module A (pushers including springs and dividers for rigid packages), Module B (hooks or rods for hanging items), Module C (smart bins or box‑on‑scale modules for bulk or amorphous goods), Module D (gated gravity feed modules), Module E (gravity trays for fragile food trays), Module F (sliding trays with low‑friction floors for soft packaging), and Module G (auto‑purge modules). Modules may be inserted, removed, and vertically repositioned relative to the transformer base, and may be simultaneously present in a single cabinet. Each module may include local sensing and/or actuation (e.g., gates, latches, motors, load cells, break‑beam sensors, RFID, and temperature sensors) that report to the cabinet controller via the vertical backbone.  Smart Bin / Box‑on‑Scale (Soft Goods). In one embodiment, a container or bin for amorphous goods (e.g., flexible milk bags) is supported on a dedicated load cell such that debiting occurs on detected weight loss. The controller may validate the removal or dispensing event using the Visual Tracking Layer and may apply anti‑fraud logic when a weight increase indicates a forced return not corroborated by computer‑vision validation.  Gravity Trays (Fragile Food). In one embodiment, a merchandising module comprises an inclined low‑friction gravity surface configured to present delicate trays (e.g., meat or salads) and to advance a next tray forward by gravity. The module includes one or more load cells to confirm removal events and includes a temperature sensor coupled to the controller. The controller implements a temperature interlock configured to inhibit sale and/or unlocking for the affected slot if a sensed shelf temperature exceeds a safety limit. 

[PAGE 5]
Module D (Gated Gravity Feed). In one embodiment, a gated gravity feed module comprises an inclined chute terminating at a locking gate and a weighing dish positioned to receive a dispensed item. The cabinet controller is configured to open the gate to dispense one item from the chute to the weighing dish, and to lock the gate while the weighing dish detects non‑zero weight. The gate remains locked until the user physically removes the item such that the weight returns to zero, thereby preventing sweeping of multiple items and enabling precise, single‑item accounting. III. Induction and Inventory Manifesting.  The system supports an “induction” process for associating physical goods with digital inventory records, including tracking tag identifiers (e.g., RFID UID), SKU identifiers, batch identifiers, and expiration data. In certain embodiments, induction may be performed locally at or near the cabinet using an induction station that reads tags and records associated product attributes. In addition or alternatively, induction may be performed remotely at an upstream supplier warehouse or distribution center, where tags are applied to goods and the tag‑to‑product association is created off‑site. In such upstream embodiments, a supplier system uploads an allow‑list or inventory manifest to a remote server, and the cabinet controller downloads the manifest prior to or upon receipt of goods so that the cabinet can recognize the pre‑tagged goods without requiring local scanning of each item. In embodiments, the cabinet controller maintains inventory state by comparing observed tag reads and/or sensor‑fusion outputs to the allow‑list manifest. When a pre‑tagged item arrives at the cabinet, the cabinet recognizes the item based on the uploaded association and transitions the item into an “active” inventory state without requiring a local induction step. Local induction may still be used for exceptions, returns to stock, or ad hoc stocking by an operator. IV. Secure Unit “A” and Dual‑Mode Operation In embodiments, a portion of the cabinet defines a secure compartment for restricted goods (e.g., tobacco or alcohol). The secure compartment may include switchable smart glass (e.g., PDLC) that is opaque in a sleep mode and transitions to transparent in an inspection mode after successful verification. Verification may include biometric verification and/or token‑based verification using a QR code from a KYC‑verified account. In embodiments, the controller enforces a triple‑sensor fusion requirement (antenna matrix + overhead imaging + load cells) for restricted goods, including to prevent partial consumption or tag swapping. A single cabinet or compartment may dynamically switch between a standard mode and a restricted mode based on detected product type, a time‑of‑day rule (e.g., automatic lockout after a specified time), and/or a remote command, without changing physical hardware. V. “No Return” Policy (Anti‑Reverse System) 

[PAGE 6]
In embodiments, the system enforces a “sales final” policy using one or more anti‑reverse mechanisms. In a first implementation, one‑way mechanical elements (e.g., ratchets, pawls, directional teeth, or stops) are integrated into pushers, hooks, and/or gravity‑feed mechanisms such that backward movement that would enable reinsertion is physically inhibited once an item has advanced past a removal threshold. In a second implementation, for shelf zones that do not employ one‑way mechanical elements (e.g., bins or sliding trays), the edge controller executes return‑detection logic using load‑cell data, break‑beam data, RFID reads, and/or the Visual Tracking Layer.  Punitive Penalty Logic. In embodiments, upon detecting an unauthorized weight change (e.g., a weight increase indicating a forced return) or a forced return attempt that is not validated by computer vision, the controller calculates and applies a punitive charge defined as a multiple of the highest‑value item currently stocked in the enclosure (or in the affected zone), thereby deterring fraud and chargebacks. The user interface presents the applicable “no return” terms and penalty logic prior to unlocking and requires an affirmative user input (e.g., selection of an “I Agree” control) before an unlock signal is issued. The system may store a time‑stamped consent record in a local and/or cloud ledger and associate the consent record with the session identifier and transaction record. VI. Auto‑Purge and Smart Waste In embodiments, the cabinet automatically clears expired or designated items to free shelf space. A purge mechanism may include a retractable front stop that drops, allowing a spring to push an item forward until it falls into a bottom compartment. The bottom compartment may include load cells and RFID sensing to track purged items as “quarantined” pending collection. In embodiments, an anti‑scavenger rule may charge a defined value (e.g., original price) if a user retrieves an item from the waste compartment. VII. User Identification, Receipting, and Offline Operation Prior to granting access, the cabinet controller may require user identification via a biometric sensor and/or scanning of an optical code generated by a mobile application. The optical code may encode or reference a session token that links to a user profile and one or more payment instruments. In embodiments, the user interface display is provided in a configuration selected from the group consisting of: (A) door‑integrated (embedded within or mounted upon the access barrier) and/or (B) boom‑mounted (mounted via an external articulating boom arm or side‑bracket allowing height/angle adjustment).  Progressive Biometric Enrollment with Global Roaming & Payment Validation. In one embodiment, a first session is authorized via a token (e.g., QR code) at a local cabinet. The system captures a facial vector (or other biometric vector) and requests explicit opt‑in consent for biometric enrollment. Upon opt‑in, the system transmits the vector to a centralized cloud 

[PAGE 7]
repository for propagation across a network of cabinets. Upon recognizing the user at a different cabinet (including in another region or country), the system automatically validates regional compatibility of the linked payment instrument. If the payment instrument is invalid for the new region, the controller inhibits access and triggers a “payment method refresh” prompt, requiring a one‑time update while retaining the biometric link. In embodiments, upon transaction capture (e.g., at the time an item removal is finalized), the transaction processing module generates a digital receipt and, where required by local tax rules, a fiscal receipt, and transmits the receipt to the user’s mobile application and/or registered email address. The cabinet user interface may present pre‑payment information including exact expiration dates, scheduled purge times, and real‑time price changes for the specific items available. In embodiments, the transaction processing module applies a real‑time incremental hold such that funds are blocked on the payment instrument in near‑real time per item removed, rather than aggregated only at session end. In embodiments, the cabinet controller maintains a robust offline financial ledger and an offline deposit buffer. If network connectivity is lost, the controller continues to authorize access based on locally stored session tokens and/or a locally stored balance, debits funds against the local ledger, and stores transaction history for asynchronous reconciliation once connectivity is restored. Accordingly, the cabinet remains operative for sales during network outages while preserving auditability and eventual settlement integrity. In embodiments, the cabinet hardware includes an integrated uninterruptible power supply (UPS) and/or battery unit mounted within the chassis and electrically coupled to at least the electronic locks, door sensors, and the cabinet controller. The UPS/battery unit is configured to maintain a secure state of the cabinet and to enable transaction continuity during external power loss, including continued acceptance of tokenized credentials, continued operation of sensors needed to detect removals, and continued recording of transaction events into the local financial ledger for asynchronous reconciliation. The UPS/battery unit may further provide controlled shutdown behavior and tamper‑event logging when backup power falls below a threshold. VIII. Pricing and Remote Controls The pricing module implements real‑time dynamic pricing for items and may update prices bidirectionally (increase or decrease) based on a machine‑learning model and/or rule engine that considers multiple inputs including: (A) expiration date timestamps and time‑to‑expiry; (B) real‑time external commodity or market values for the item category; (C) demand and surge logic based on traffic levels, scarcity, or inventory velocity; and (D) a location profile defining base pricing tiers dependent on physical deployment location (e.g., airport vs. residential) and local purchasing power. The controller may publish updated prices to the shelf‑edge information system and/or user interface and may store pricing decisions and inputs in an auditable log. IX. Multi‑Tenant Shelf Rental and Split Payments 

[PAGE 8]
In embodiments, the cabinet is configured as a multi‑tenant platform in which defined shelves, slots, or module zones are virtually allocated (e.g., leased) to different suppliers. The controller associates each stocked item and/or slot with a tenant identifier and maintains per‑tenant inventory visibility and controls. A remote supplier interface enables a supplier to monitor inventory on its allocated shelves, receive alerts (e.g., low stock, approaching purge), and dynamically update prices and promotions for its own products in real time, independent of other tenants.  Multi‑Merchant Transaction Splitting (Hardware‑Triggered). In embodiments, the transaction processing module performs automated settlement routing in which a single user basket/session is split into multiple settlement legs to different merchant accounts. The split is hardware‑triggered by a shelf‑specific sensor identifier (e.g., shelf module ID and/or zone sensor ID) associated with a respective merchant account, and is performed automatically and invisibly to the user (i.e., not a user‑selected “split bill” option). The system may still present a unified user experience and consolidated receipt while routing settlement proceeds to the appropriate merchant accounts. X. Clustered Deployment (Master‑Slave) In embodiments, the system comprises a plurality of physical storage units coupled to a shared controller in a master‑slave configuration. A master unit may house higher‑cost components such as an AI processor, payment terminal, biometric scanner, and/or fiscal printer logic, while one or more adjacent slave units provide additional storage volume and include sensors and locks that report to, and are controlled by, the master unit. In embodiments, slave units may optionally include auxiliary displays (e.g., for advertising, wayfinding, or navigation) even when payment capture and primary user authentication are centralized at the master unit. The shared controller maintains a single user session and a “virtual basket” across multiple enclosures such that a user may authenticate at the master unit, retrieve items from multiple slave units, and receive a single consolidated receipt and settlement for the session. In certain embodiments, transaction settlement is performed according to a real‑time incremental authorization scheme that supports both standalone and clustered deployments while reducing per‑item transaction fees. In a first mode (Instant Capture), the system captures funds substantially immediately upon confirmation of an item‑removal event for a given cabinet session. In a second mode (Aggregated Capture), upon each confirmed item‑removal event the system (i) records a legally binding purchase commitment for the item in a local and/or cloud ledger and (ii) increments an authorization hold or pre‑authorization amount associated with the user’s payment instrument, without necessarily capturing funds at the payment gateway for each individual item. The system then triggers a consolidated capture at session termination (e.g., upon door closure, user checkout action, timeout, or departure from a cluster) to settle a single aggregated transaction reflecting items removed from one or more cabinets in the cluster. Abstract A retail cabinet includes an enclosure with an access barrier and a reconfigurable chassis supporting vertically repositionable merchandising modules coupled to a controller via an adjustable power/data interface. The chassis includes an active motorized leveling system with an inclinometer and motorized feet actuators that automatically level the cabinet to a true horizon and inhibit transactions when tilt exceeds a threshold, thereby improving weighing accuracy and reducing fraud. A sensor fusion architecture includes an always‑present internal camera layer for continuous visual tracking, combined with a selectable physical detection layer: high‑value zones use load cells and RFID, while standard zones use break‑beam sensors and mechanical anti‑reverse inhibitors. Shelf‑edge electronic displays present real‑time bidirectional pricing and item‑specific batch metadata (including expiration dates) and automatically update using FIFO queue logic upon item removal. The system supports tokenized access, progressive biometric enrollment with cloud synchronization and regional payment validation, automated multi‑merchant settlement splitting triggered by shelf sensor identifiers, and punitive penalty logic that applies charges upon unauthorized returns.

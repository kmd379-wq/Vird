SMART RETAIL CABINET WITH TOKENIZED ACCESS AND SPLIT PAYMENTS

Field of the Invention
The present disclosure relates generally to autonomous retail and smart vending systems, and more particularly to modular, reconfigurable storage cabinets configured to dispense and account for goods—including perishable and restricted goods—using sensor fusion, dynamic pricing, and secure access controls. In example embodiments, the disclosure further relates to networked transaction processing, fiscal receipt generation, and multi‑tenant inventory architectures in which different suppliers may control pricing and settlement for goods stored within shared cabinet infrastructure, including clustered deployments comprising multiple enclosures coupled to a shared controller.
Background
Conventional vending machines and “grab‑and‑go” cabinets typically rely on fixed shelving layouts and limited sensing, which can reduce merchandising flexibility and increase shrink when packaging types vary or when customers attempt to remove multiple items at once. These limitations are amplified for perishable goods whose value changes over time, and for restricted goods (e.g., alcohol or tobacco) that require age/identity controls and auditability. Many systems also become inoperative during network outages, or cannot support transparent, item‑level pricing and accounting in real time.
Additionally, modern retail deployments increasingly involve multiple suppliers that wish to stock products in shared physical footprints while retaining control over pricing, promotions, inventory visibility, and settlement. Existing architectures often cannot route proceeds to different payees on an item‑by‑item basis or generate jurisdiction‑specific fiscal receipts immediately at capture. Accordingly, there is a need for a modular cabinet architecture that enables rapid shelf reconfiguration, reliable single‑item dispensing and accounting, secure yet flexible user identification (including tokenized access), resilient offline transaction handling, and scalable multi‑cabinet installations with shared resources.

Detailed Description
In one embodiment, and with reference to FIG. 1, a universal smart cabinet system is provided for autonomous retail of goods, including perishable items and restricted items. The system comprises at least one enclosure defining a storage volume and at least one access barrier (e.g., a door) configured to be selectively locked and unlocked under control of a cabinet controller. The enclosure supports a plurality of interchangeable merchandising modules that may be rearranged without tool‑intensive rework to accommodate different product form factors. In certain embodiments, the cabinet operates as a node of a networked platform and/or as part of a clustered installation comprising multiple enclosures that share one or more processing or transaction resources, as described further below.
I. Hardware Chassis (Transformer Base)
In embodiments, the unified chassis includes a vertical power and data backbone defining conductive rails or busbars along a rear wall, such that shelves and/or modules couple to the backbone to receive power and exchange data. This enables instant vertical reconfiguration of shelves and modules while maintaining electrical connectivity and sensor communication. In certain embodiments, the chassis further includes a rear‑loading configuration enabling first‑in‑first‑out (FIFO) stocking by permitting the cabinet to be undocked from a wall or fixture for access to a rear loading face.
In embodiments, the cabinet further includes a mobility system comprising industrial casters for rolling movement and retractable leveling feet for stabilization. The mobility system enables a service operator to reposition the cabinet for maintenance, cleaning, and rear loading, while the leveling feet provide a stable and repeatable reference plane for weight‑based sensing and dispensing operations.
In embodiments, each module includes a smart edge along a user‑facing front portion of a shelf or module. The smart edge may include a high‑density antenna matrix (i.e., an array of antennas rather than a single antenna) and an LED information strip. The antenna matrix and LED strip cooperate to (i) detect tagged items within defined pick zones, (ii) indicate item locations and dynamic prices, and (iii) provide user navigation and status feedback during a shopping session.
With reference to FIG. 2, the system further includes a processing system implemented by the cabinet controller and/or a remote server. The processing system may include (i) a sensor‑fusion module configured to combine signals from the antenna matrix, an overhead imaging sensor, and one or more load cells or weighing structures to determine item removals, returns, and partial‑consumption behaviors; (ii) a pricing module configured to compute dynamic prices for items based on time‑based expiration curves, sensory quality degradation, and/or slot stagnation; and (iii) a transaction processing module configured to establish a user session, reserve funds via a pre‑authorization, capture payment, generate a digital and/or fiscal receipt, and record item‑level events in a local ledger for audit and reconciliation. In one example, the user session may be initiated by biometric authentication and/or by scanning an optical code (e.g., QR code or barcode) generated by a mobile application, wherein the optical code encodes or references a session token used to request a payment pre‑authorization and to authorize subsequent access without requiring biometric capture at the cabinet. The transaction processing module may further support split settlement in which proceeds for different items within a single session are routed to different payees, such as a cabinet operator and one or more third‑party suppliers, as described further below.
II. Module Variety (Interchangeable)
The cabinet may be populated with a plurality of different merchandising modules, including, by way of example: Module A (pushers including springs and dividers for rigid packages), Module B (hooks or rods for hanging items), Module C (box‑on‑scale modules for bulk items), Module D (gated gravity feed modules), Module F (sliding trays with low‑friction floors for soft packaging), and Module G (auto‑purge modules). Modules may be inserted, removed, and vertically repositioned relative to the transformer base, and may be simultaneously present in a single cabinet. Each module may include local sensing and/or actuation (e.g., gates, latches, motors, load cells) that report to the cabinet controller via the vertical backbone.
Module D (Gated Gravity Feed). In one embodiment, a gated gravity feed module comprises an inclined chute terminating at a locking gate and a weighing dish positioned to receive a dispensed item. The cabinet controller is configured to open the gate to dispense one item from the chute to the weighing dish, and to lock the gate while the weighing dish detects non‑zero weight. The gate remains locked until the user physically removes the item such that the weight returns to zero, thereby preventing sweeping of multiple items and enabling precise, single‑item accounting.
III. Induction and Inventory Manifesting. 
The system supports an “induction” process for associating physical goods with digital inventory records, including tracking tag identifiers (e.g., RFID UID), SKU identifiers, batch identifiers, and expiration data. In certain embodiments, induction may be performed locally at or near the cabinet using an induction station that reads tags and records associated product attributes. In addition or alternatively, induction may be performed remotely at an upstream supplier warehouse or distribution center, where tags are applied to goods and the tag‑to‑product association is created off‑site. In such upstream embodiments, a supplier system uploads an allow‑list or inventory manifest to a remote server, and the cabinet controller downloads the manifest prior to or upon receipt of goods so that the cabinet can recognize the pre‑tagged goods without requiring local scanning of each item.
In embodiments, the cabinet controller maintains inventory state by comparing observed tag reads and/or sensor‑fusion outputs to the allow‑list manifest. When a pre‑tagged item arrives at the cabinet, the cabinet recognizes the item based on the uploaded association and transitions the item into an “active” inventory state without requiring a local induction step. Local induction may still be used for exceptions, returns to stock, or ad hoc stocking by an operator.
IV. Secure Unit “A” and Dual‑Mode Operation
In embodiments, a portion of the cabinet defines a secure compartment for restricted goods (e.g., tobacco or alcohol). The secure compartment may include switchable smart glass (e.g., PDLC) that is opaque in a sleep mode and transitions to transparent in an inspection mode after successful verification. Verification may include biometric verification and/or token‑based verification using a QR code from a KYC‑verified account. In embodiments, the controller enforces a triple‑sensor fusion requirement (antenna matrix + overhead imaging + load cells) for restricted goods, including to prevent partial consumption or tag swapping. A single cabinet or compartment may dynamically switch between a standard mode and a restricted mode based on detected product type, a time‑of‑day rule (e.g., automatic lockout after a specified time), and/or a remote command, without changing physical hardware.
V. “No Return” Policy (Anti‑Reverse System)
In embodiments, the system enforces “sales final” using mechanical and/or software techniques. Mechanically, pushers and hooks may include a one‑way ratchet or stop that physically prevents backward movement. For weight‑based zones (e.g., bins or trays) that allow bidirectional movement, the controller may detect a weight increase indicative of a return and apply a penalty (e.g., a double‑charge) only after presenting an affirmative consent control to the user prior to door unlock.
VI. Auto‑Purge and Smart Waste
In embodiments, the cabinet automatically clears expired or designated items to free shelf space. A purge mechanism may include a retractable front stop that drops, allowing a spring to push an item forward until it falls into a bottom compartment. The bottom compartment may include load cells and RFID sensing to track purged items as “quarantined” pending collection. In embodiments, an anti‑scavenger rule may charge a defined value (e.g., original price) if a user retrieves an item from the waste compartment.
VII. User Identification, Receipting, and Offline Operation
Prior to granting access, the cabinet controller may require user identification via a biometric sensor and/or scanning of an optical code generated by a mobile application. The optical code may encode or reference a session token that links to a user profile and one or more payment instruments. The transaction processing module may reserve funds via pre‑authorization based on the session token, and may verify that the identity associated with the selected payment instrument matches the registered user profile (e.g., via KYC and/or issuer verification) to reduce fraud. In embodiments, scanning the optical code from a KYC‑verified account constitutes sufficient age verification for restricted goods, thereby allowing restricted‑mode access without mandatory camera capture in certain jurisdictions or configurations.
In embodiments, upon transaction capture (e.g., at the time an item removal is finalized), the transaction processing module generates a digital receipt and, where required by local tax rules, a fiscal receipt, and transmits the receipt to the user’s mobile application and/or registered email address. The cabinet user interface may present pre‑payment information including exact expiration dates, scheduled purge times, and real‑time price changes for the specific items available. In embodiments, the transaction processing module applies a real‑time incremental hold such that funds are blocked on the payment instrument in near‑real time per item removed, rather than aggregated only at session end.
In embodiments, the cabinet controller maintains a robust offline financial ledger and an offline deposit buffer. If network connectivity is lost, the controller continues to authorize access based on locally stored session tokens and/or a locally stored balance, debits funds against the local ledger, and stores transaction history for asynchronous reconciliation once connectivity is restored. Accordingly, the cabinet remains operative for sales during network outages while preserving auditability and eventual settlement integrity.
VIII. Pricing and Remote Controls
The pricing module may implement time‑based decay, sensory decay based on detected quality loss, and space‑yield discounts based on slot stagnation. Pricing rules may apply across goods categories including food and non‑food items. In embodiments, pricing rules and thresholds may be updated remotely, including by an operator console and/or by supplier consoles associated with multi‑tenant shelf rental, as described below.
IX. Multi‑Tenant Shelf Rental and Split Payments
In embodiments, the cabinet is configured as a multi‑tenant platform in which defined shelves, slots, or module zones are virtually allocated (e.g., leased) to different suppliers. The controller associates each stocked item and/or slot with a tenant identifier and maintains per‑tenant inventory visibility and controls. A remote supplier interface enables a supplier to monitor inventory on its allocated shelves, receive alerts (e.g., low stock, approaching purge), and dynamically update prices and promotions for its own products in real time, independent of other tenants. In embodiments, the transaction processing module performs basket splitting such that a single user session is divided into per‑tenant settlement legs, routing funds for each removed item (or item group) to a corresponding merchant account (e.g., cabinet operator account and/or supplier account), while presenting a unified user experience and consolidated receipt.
X. Clustered Deployment (Master‑Slave)
In embodiments, the system comprises a plurality of physical storage units coupled to a shared controller in a master‑slave configuration. A master unit may house higher‑cost components such as an AI processor, payment terminal, biometric scanner, and/or fiscal printer logic, while one or more adjacent slave units provide additional storage volume and include sensors and locks that report to, and are controlled by, the master unit. The shared controller maintains a single user session and a “virtual basket” across multiple enclosures such that a user may authenticate (e.g., by scanning a QR code) at the master unit, retrieve items from multiple slave units, and receive a single consolidated fiscal receipt and payment capture for the session. This architecture reduces per‑cabinet bill‑of‑materials cost while enabling scalable multi‑unit installations at a single location.

Abstract
A modular autonomous retail cabinet includes an enclosure with an access barrier, a reconfigurable chassis having a vertical power/data backbone, and interchangeable merchandising modules including pushers, hooks, box‑on‑scale modules, and gated gravity feed modules. A sensor suite performs sensor fusion using RFID antenna arrays, imaging, and load cells to determine item removal events and enforce single‑item dispensing. A controller establishes a user session via biometric input and/or scanning of an optical code from a mobile application, reserves funds via pre‑authorization, computes prices using expiration data, captures payment, and generates fiscal receipts. Inventory induction may be performed upstream by associating tag identifiers with product data and synchronizing an inventory manifest to the cabinet. The controller supports multi‑tenant shelf allocation, item‑level split settlement to multiple payees, offline ledger operation with asynchronous reconciliation, mode switching between standard and restricted operation, and clustered deployments with master‑slave cabinets sharing a virtual basket and consolidated checkout.

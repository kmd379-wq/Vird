Autonomous Retail Ecosystem with AI-Driven Dynamic Pricing and Biometric Access Control for Restricted Goods  Field of the Invention The present invention relates to automated retail systems, and more particularly to an autonomous retail ecosystem that utilizes radio-frequency identification (RFID), computer vision, biometric sensing, and artificial intelligence (AI) to manage inventory, perform dynamic pricing for perishable goods, and control access to restricted goods such as alcohol and tobacco through biometric age verification and pre-authorized security deposits. Background Conventional vending machines and automated retail cabinets are typically configured either for low-security food and beverage sales or for human-attended point-of-sale environments. They generally lack the ability to: (i) autonomously adjust pricing for perishable goods based on real-time expiration data and sales velocity; and (ii) lawfully sell age-restricted products without an on-site attendant. Known systems that attempt dynamic pricing often rely on centralized pricing rules that ignore item-level expiration dates and do not integrate physical-shelf sensing. Known systems that address age-restricted sales typically depend on human ID verification at the point of sale, or on simple age gates that are easily circumvented. These limitations lead to high food waste, reduced revenue, inability to monetize restricted goods in unattended environments, and increased compliance risk. There is therefore a need for an integrated, hardware-anchored system that couples an intelligent shelf architecture with AI-driven pricing logic for perishable items, and that further integrates biometric age verification and financial pre-authorization to enable secure and compliant autonomous sale of restricted goods, while operating in both a standard food mode and a high-security restricted-goods mode. Detailed Description In one embodiment, and with reference to FIG. 1, an autonomous retail system comprises a secure enclosure having an interior volume accessible through at least one door, an access-control mechanism coupled to the door, a plurality of smart shelves arranged within the interior volume, an exterior user interface, and a biometric camera mounted on or near the front of the cabinet. The exterior user interface may include a touch-sensitive display and/or physical buttons that allow a user to authenticate, request cabinet access, view pricing information, and confirm purchase operations. The biometric camera (also referred to as “The Eye”) is positioned so that it can capture facial image data of users in front of the cabinet and, in some implementations, also observe the interior region near the front of the shelves. The front edge of each shelf defines a linear “front zone” region where a corresponding antenna array is positioned, as generally illustrated in FIG. 1. With reference to FIG. 2, the system further includes a processing system implemented as an edge controller or edge AI unit located in an electronics bay within or adjacent to the cabinet. The edge controller includes at least one processor, memory, and one or more machine-readable instruction sets that implement a pricing engine, an inventory-valuation module, a biometric analysis module, an access-control module, a transaction-processing module, and a sensor-fusion module. The edge controller is communicatively coupled to the various sensors (including the cameras and RFID antennas), to the access-control mechanism, and, when available, to a remote server via a network interface (e.g., LTE, Wi-Fi, or Ethernet). As shown schematically in FIG. 2, the edge controller maintains a local cache or database of inventory, pricing, user deposit, and transaction data, and may be powered in part by a backup or uninterruptible power supply so that core functions can continue during power or network disruptions. In particular, basic functions such as unlocking and locking the door, counting items, updating inventory, and debiting funds against a locally maintained deposit or balance can be carried out autonomously via local caching on the edge controller even when a cloud AI service or external network connection is unavailable, thereby preventing the cabinet from becoming inoperative in the event of an internet outage.

With reference to FIG. 3, each smart shelf incorporates a sensing geometry that includes a linear front-zone antenna array and a camera field of view that overlaps the area in which users’ hands interact with products. The smart shelf may comprise a matrix of segmented near-field RFID antennas disposed under the product-support surface, with at least one linear antenna array positioned along the front zone, spring-loaded pushers for advancing products toward the front of the shelf, and addressable LED strips associated with shelf zones corresponding to individual product positions. Each product placed on the shelves includes a tag encoding at least a unique product identifier and, for perishable goods, an expiration date or a link to expiration data stored in an associated database. The camera is oriented so that its viewing cone covers the same general region as the front-zone antenna array, creating a sensor-fusion region in which both visual evidence of hand/object movement and RFID evidence of tag presence or removal are available. The sensor-fusion module executed by the edge controller combines camera outputs with RFID readings from the smart shelves to improve detection robustness. For example, if the camera detects that a user’s hand has picked up an object from a particular shelf zone within the overlap region, but the corresponding RFID antennas do not detect removal of an expected tag (for example, due to RF shielding by foil packaging), the system treats this as an anomalous event. In response, the edge controller may trigger an audible or visual alarm, temporarily lock or refuse to release the door for subsequent sessions, hold or increase the user’s deposit, flag the transaction for review, or otherwise restrict access. This sensor fusion between camera data and RFID data makes the system more resistant to common theft attempts that rely on blocking, shielding, or spoofing RFID signals. With reference to FIG. 4, the system further includes an induction station used for associating product information with unique RFID identifiers prior to stocking the cabinet. The induction station may be embodied as a fixed docking station or as a handheld scanner device that is communicatively coupled to the edge controller. In an example induction workflow, an employee picks up a new batch of perishable items, scans a barcode or other SKU code (for example, recognizing the item as milk), then scans or programs the RFID tag that will be attached to each item, and enters or confirms the expiration date in an associated application running on the induction station. The system thus stores an association between the SKU, the expiration date, and the unique RFID identifier for each item before the item is placed on the smart shelves. This “Association of SKU and Expiration Data with a Unique RFID Identifier Prior to Stocking” ensures that, when the item later appears on the shelf and is detected by the front-zone antenna array, the edge controller can immediately resolve both product identity and expiration information by reading the RFID tag. By combining the pre-association performed at the induction station (FIG. 4) with the per-position sensing on the smart shelves and the sensor-fusion region (FIG. 3), the system maintains an accurate, item-level view of inventory and remaining shelf life. The edge controller can use this information to implement a perishable-goods dynamic pricing embodiment in a food-mode configuration. In such an embodiment, the access-control mechanism may be configured either to allow door opening upon basic payment authorization or deposit, or to remain unlocked in monitored environments. The pricing engine obtains expiration data for each perishable item based on the association stored during the induction process and/or by reading RFID-encoded fields, and may further query a product database using the product identifier if additional

metadata is needed. Because the expiration data is bound to the unique RFID tag before stocking, the system can reliably track remaining shelf life even when items are moved or re-shelved. The pricing engine further determines sales velocity for each item or SKU based on historical transaction logs stored in local or cloud memory, including at least time-stamped removal events detected by the RFID antenna matrix and, in some embodiments, corroborated by camera detections within the sensor-fusion region. Using these inputs, the pricing engine calculates a dynamic price as a function of remaining shelf life, current inventory levels, and one or more operator-defined policies specifying decay curves or discount thresholds. Dynamic pricing decisions can be computed locally by the edge controller so that, even if cloud-based analytics are temporarily unavailable, the system continues to apply and display appropriate discounts based on locally cached rules and data. For example, when an item enters a predefined near-expiry window, the pricing engine may apply a discount that increases as the expiration date approaches, subject to minimum and maximum price constraints. The updated price is stored in an item-pricing record maintained on the edge controller and communicated to the user interface and, optionally when connectivity exists, to a remote management portal. To guide users to discounted items, the system activates the addressable LED strip associated with the shelf zone of the discounted item, for example by illuminating the LED in a distinct color or pattern to distinguish discounted items from full-price items. In an embodiment directed to restricted goods such as alcohol or tobacco, the system operates in a restricted-mode configuration in which the access-control mechanism remains locked until completion of a biometric age-verification protocol and a pre-authorization deposit process. When a user approaches the unit, the biometric interface (FIG. 1) captures facial image data. The biometric analysis module executed on the edge controller runs one or more AI models to produce an estimated age of the user, optionally along with a confidence measure, and/or to match the captured face to a previously verified identity associated with a user account in a companion mobile application or backend Know Your Customer (KYC) database. When the estimated age and confidence satisfy a configured threshold (for example, estimated age greater than or equal to a legal minimum age such as twenty-one years with confidence above a predetermined threshold), or when a matching user account is found with verified age, the access-control module determines that the user is eligible to purchase restricted goods and initiates a payment or deposit handshake. If the user is determined to be underage or unverified, the access-control module denies access, and the user interface displays a rejection message. Optional logging and alerting functions may record underage access attempts. For an eligible user, the transaction-processing module initiates a security-deposit logic flow in cooperation with a payment gateway or, in offline mode, with a locally managed deposit buffer. In one implementation, the user presents a payment credential via the companion app, a contactless payment card, or a digital wallet, and selects an “Unlock” or similar command via the display or app. The system computes a deposit amount based on a risk profile associated with the user and/or the category of goods. The risk profile may consider prior transaction history, chargeback incidents, or operator-defined rules. When online, the payment gateway is requested to place a pre-authorization hold on the user’s funding source for at least the computed deposit amount, such as fifty dollars for high-value items. When offline, the edge controller records a

provisional debit against a locally cached deposit or tokenized credential and stores the transaction for later reconciliation. Only upon receiving confirmation that the pre-authorization was successful or that a sufficient local deposit buffer exists does the access-control module energize a solenoid lock or other actuator to release the door. The system may provide a visual or audible indication that the door is unlocked. Once the door is opened, the user may remove one or more restricted items. The smart shelf’s RFID antennas detect removal by sensing absence of previously present tags, and determine which specific items and quantities have been removed based on tag identifiers and zone information. In embodiments employing the sensor-fusion configuration of FIG. 3, camera detections of hand movements and object removal are correlated with RFID events within the front-zone region to detect discrepancies indicative of tampering, shielding, or attempted theft. When the door is closed or when a predefined timeout occurs, the inventory-valuation module finalizes the list of items taken, and the transaction-processing module calculates a final transaction amount based on the current price of each removed item. For example, if the user removes a bottle of whiskey priced at forty-five dollars, the system captures a forty-five dollar charge against the pre-authorization and releases the remaining balance of the deposit hold when online, or records a forty-five dollar charge against the local deposit buffer and adjusts the remaining offline balance when offline. If no items are taken, the full pre-authorization hold is released or the local deposit buffer is restored. The system can also support mixed-mode transactions in which both perishable food items and restricted goods are present in the same cabinet, with the operating mode, deposit logic, and sensor-fusion thresholds tailored based on the set of items detected as present or as enabled for sale in a given session. A mode state is stored in the processing system and optionally controlled by a remote management server. This dual-mode behavior is implemented in software as a state machine that, in each state, configures device drivers, pricing logic, biometric requirements, deposit thresholds, and offline/online behaviors accordingly. Because the claimed system is anchored in specific physical components, as illustrated in FIGS. 1–4—including the secure enclosure, induction station for associating SKU and expiration data with unique RFID identifiers, front-zone antenna arrays, camera-based sensor-fusion geometry, edge controller with local caching, and access-control hardware—and implements concrete, technical algorithms for sensor fusion, dynamic pricing, offline transaction handling, and age-restricted access control, it provides technical improvements over generic retail computers, including reduced spoilage, improved fraud resistance, and resilience to network outages. Abstract An autonomous retail system combines RFID-based sensing, camera-based monitoring, and edge computing to enable secure, offline-capable sale of perishable and restricted goods. Items are first processed at an induction station where a barcode is scanned, an RFID tag is scanned or programmed, and an expiration date is entered, creating an association between SKU, expiration data, and a unique RFID identifier. Within a secure cabinet, linear “front zone” antenna arrays and a camera field of view overlap to form a sensor-fusion region that detects hand movements and item removal, enabling anti-theft logic even when RFID is blocked. An edge controller performs local inventory tracking, dynamic pricing based on expiration and sales velocity, biometric age verification, and deposit-based access control, operating from a local cache when cloud or network connectivity is unavailable.

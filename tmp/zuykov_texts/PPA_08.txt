INTELLIGENT BULK PRODUCE QUALITY CONTROL AND DYNAMIC PRICING SYSTEM

FIELD
[0001] The present disclosure relates to bulk-storage vending machines, unattended retail systems, produce vending, load-cell weighing, customer-managed product disposal, spoilage tracking, dynamic pricing, hopper geometry, automatic hopper product leveling, and control systems for perishable loose goods.
BACKGROUND
[0002] Bulk-storage vending machines and unattended produce dispensers may store loose fruits, vegetables, or other perishable products in open or semi-open hoppers. A customer may inspect products before completing a purchase. In conventional systems, if a customer encounters spoiled, bruised, overripe, undersized, damaged, or otherwise unacceptable products, the machine often lacks a reliable automated mechanism for allowing a limited replacement while preventing abuse.
[0003] Existing vending systems may use inventory counters, timers, RFID tags, shelf sensors, or cameras to estimate inventory status or product freshness. However, these systems may not directly measure the physical quantity of products that customers reject or dispose of during actual transactions. Without a measured disposal signal, dynamic pricing may be based on time or inventory assumptions rather than on a physical spoilage indicator specific to the current product batch.
[0004] In addition, bulk produce hoppers create weighing and flow problems. Loose produce can bridge, pile unevenly, roll to side walls, or apply eccentric loads to the weighing structure. Multi-load-cell platforms can increase cost and calibration complexity, while flat-bottomed or poorly shaped hoppers may not reliably direct product mass toward a central measurement point. Uneven product distribution can reduce gravimetric accuracy and may interfere with consistent customer access to products.
[0005] A need exists for a vending machine that integrates customer disposal tracking, abuse prevention, dual weight accounting, and AI-based dynamic pricing with a hopper structure that improves gravity-fed flow and weighing accuracy. A further need exists for an internal automatic product-leveling system within the hopper, a conical hopper bottom configured to direct produce toward a weighing point, and a single load cell architecture that reduces complexity while supporting accurate inventory and spoilage calculations.

A. Bulk-Storage Vending Machine Architecture
[0022] In some embodiments, a vending machine includes a chassis, a storage hopper, a disposal bin, a central controller, a customer display, a payment terminal, and an AI pricing module. The storage hopper stores loose perishable products in a customer-accessible or machine-dispensed configuration. The disposal bin is positioned near the hopper, such as at hand level or adjacent to a customer inspection region, so that a customer can place spoiled or unacceptable products into the disposal bin during a transaction.
[0023] The disposal bin may include a load cell, platform scale, beam scale, or other weighing sensor. The disposal bin may also include a motorized lid, one-way slot, valve, RFID/NFC lock, camera, status indicator, LED strip, or retrieval-prevention structure. The disposal bin communicates with the central controller through an analog-to-digital converter, I2C, SPI, UART, USB, CAN, wireless connection, or other interface.
[0024] The central controller may be an embedded microcontroller, single-board computer, industrial controller, or other computing system. The controller receives disposal bin weight data, hopper weight data, product count data, payment data, customer input, and sensor data. The controller maintains transaction state, applies replacement threshold rules, updates display information, communicates with the AI pricing module, and forwards charge amounts to the payment terminal.
B. Customer Replacement and Disposal Workflow
[0025] In some embodiments, a transaction begins when a customer initiates a session using a card, NFC credential, QR code, mobile application, biometric credential, keypad input, or other authentication mechanism. At the beginning of the transaction, the controller resets a disposal counter and a session disposal weight register.
[0026] The customer may remove an item from the hopper and inspect the item. If the item is acceptable, the customer pays for the item or adds it to a virtual basket. If the item is spoiled, damaged, overripe, bruised, moldy, discolored, undersized, or otherwise unacceptable, the customer places the item into the disposal bin. The disposal bin load cell measures an item weight w_i, and the controller increments a disposal counter n for the transaction.
[0027] If the disposal counter n is less than or equal to a threshold N, the controller may classify the disposal event as an accepted replacement event. The display may show a message such as replacement accepted, disposal accepted, or replacement available. The customer may then select a replacement item without being charged for the disposed item.
[0028] If the disposal counter n exceeds the threshold N, the controller may classify the session as an abuse condition. In response, the controller may charge the customer for all disposed items, charge only excess disposed items, require attendant review, lock the disposal bin, block further replacement, generate an alert, or apply another operator-configurable abuse rule. A display or LED indicator may change from a normal indication to a warning indication when the threshold is exceeded.
[0029] The threshold N may be fixed, such as N=1 or N=2, or may be adaptive. For example, the controller or AI module may increase N for an end-of-batch clearance mode, a known high-spoilage batch, a promotional mode, or a high-trust customer profile. The system may reduce N when abuse patterns, anomalous disposal weights, or repeated suspicious transactions are detected.
C. Dual Weight Accounting
[0030] In some embodiments, the system maintains at least two independent registers. A hopper inventory register represents an accounting weight of sellable product in the hopper. A disposal register represents a cumulative weight of products disposed of by customers during a batch, time interval, transaction group, or other accounting period.
[0031] When a customer places a spoiled item into the disposal bin, the disposal register increases by the measured disposal weight. The hopper inventory register is not automatically decreased as a result of that disposal event. This separation allows the system to distinguish sold product, physically disposed product, and remaining inventory for revenue calculation, spoilage tracking, and operator reporting.
[0032] The hopper inventory register may be adjusted when products are replenished, when products are sold, when an operator performs a verified inventory adjustment, when a mechanical purge is performed, or when a calibration procedure is completed. The disposal register may increase with each disposal event and may be reset or archived when a batch is replaced, a reporting interval closes, or an operator clears the batch.
D. AI Spoilage Index and Dynamic Pricing
[0033] In some embodiments, the AI pricing module receives a disposal weight W_disposal, a batch load weight W_load, a number of disposed units N_disposal, a batch loading time T_load, a base price, historical sales data, and optional image-based or sensor-based quality data. The AI pricing module computes a spoilage index according to S = W_disposal / W_load x 100, or according to another physical spoilage measure based on disposed product weight.
[0034] The AI pricing module may compare the spoilage index to one or more thresholds. For example, a spoilage index from 0 to 5 percent may produce no price change, a spoilage index from 5 to 15 percent may produce a moderate discount, a spoilage index from 15 to 30 percent may produce a larger discount and operator notification, and a spoilage index above 30 percent may produce a large discount, load block, or batch replacement recommendation.
[0035] The AI pricing module may also use rolling averages, average disposed item weight, anomaly detection, transaction frequency, time since loading, product type, temperature, humidity, camera classification, spectral analysis, customer replacement history, and operator inputs. The resulting price may be displayed on a customer display, an electronic shelf label, a remote dashboard, or a payment terminal.
[0036] In some embodiments, dynamic pricing is tied to a physical measurement of product spoilage rather than solely to time, inventory count, expiration date, or operator preference. This provides a technical link between physical disposal events, measured disposal weight, computed spoilage state, and automated price control.
E. Hopper With Conical 45-Degree Bottom
[0037] In some embodiments, the storage hopper includes an upper receiving region and a lower conical hopper bottom. The conical hopper bottom may be frustoconical, funnel-shaped, pyramidal, faceted, curved, polygonal, circular, elliptical, or otherwise tapered. The conical hopper bottom directs loose products toward a central weighing region or dispensing region by gravity.
[0038] In one embodiment, the conical hopper bottom forms an angle of about 45 degrees relative to horizontal. In other embodiments, the angle may be about 35 degrees to about 60 degrees, about 40 degrees to about 50 degrees, or any angle selected to promote gravity-fed product flow for a selected product type. The angle may be selected based on product friction, product roundness, average product size, moisture level, liner material, slope contamination, humidity, or cleaning requirements.
[0039] The conical bottom may reduce product stagnation, bridging, product pile-up, eccentric loading, and sidewall loading. In some embodiments, the conical bottom cooperates with a low-friction liner, food-safe coating, antimicrobial surface, replaceable insert, drainage channel, cleaning port, or vibration member to improve flow and sanitation.
F. Single Load Cell Weighing Architecture
[0040] In some embodiments, a single central load cell is positioned to measure product supported by the hopper. The load cell may be positioned at or below an apex region of the conical bottom, below a floating hopper base, below a central platform, through a single-point suspension, or under a load-transfer plate. The single load cell may be a beam load cell, single-point load cell, compression load cell, shear beam load cell, S-type load cell, strain gauge sensor, capacitive force sensor, piezoelectric sensor, or other weight sensor.
[0041] The conical hopper bottom directs product mass toward the single central load cell. By reducing eccentric load conditions, the hopper geometry can reduce or eliminate the need for multiple load cells at multiple corners. The single-load-cell architecture may reduce calibration complexity, cost, wiring, drift, and maintenance burden.
[0042] In some embodiments, the load cell is vibration-isolated from the chassis using dampers, elastomeric mounts, a floating bin mount, suspension members, or isolation pads. The controller may apply filtering, shock detection, door-slam rejection, stabilization delays, auto-tare, and calibration routines to improve measurement accuracy. The load cell may measure initial hopper load, remaining hopper inventory, dispensing weight, purged weight, or a combination thereof.
G. Automatic Product-Leveling System Within the Hopper
[0043] In some embodiments, the hopper includes an automatic product-leveling system positioned within the hopper. The product-leveling system acts directly on the product bed and is distinct from leveling of the cabinet chassis. The product-leveling system may redistribute loose products to maintain a desired product profile, prevent product bridges, reduce high mounds, reduce sidewall bias, and guide product toward the conical bottom and the single load cell.
[0044] The automatic product-leveling system may include one or more motorized sweep arms, rotating paddles, low-speed agitators, flexible wipers, oscillating bars, vibrating plates, eccentric vibration motors, air pulse nozzles, augers, movable floor plates, tilting baffles, controlled shutters, product rakes, linear actuators, servo-driven leveling blades, or combinations thereof. A leveling member may sweep across the product surface, move below the product surface, vibrate a wall or base, pulse air through a nozzle, or reposition a movable baffle.
[0045] The controller may activate the product-leveling system in response to image-detected mounding, load-cell instability, sidewall contact detection, hopper-level sensor imbalance, blocked flow, product bridging, repeated dispensing errors, end-of-batch conditions, customer selection, or restocking. The controller may use a camera, depth sensor, ultrasonic sensor, time-of-flight sensor, weight signal variance, motor current, product-flow sensor, or machine-learning model to determine when leveling is required.
[0046] The controller may coordinate product leveling with weighing. For example, the controller may pause weight readings while an actuator moves, wait for vibration to settle, perform auto-tare or stabilization, and then resume weighing. In other embodiments, the controller analyzes load-cell signal changes during leveling to detect product flow resistance, bridging, product clumping, product quantity, or spoilage state.
H. Displays and User Interfaces
[0047] In some embodiments, the display is a touchscreen, keypad display, LCD, TFT, LED display, e-paper display, electronic shelf label, or exterior monitor. The display may simultaneously show current unit price, weight of disposed items in the current session, transaction status, remaining replacement allowance, abuse warning, discount level, spoilage status, and operator messages.
[0048] The system may update the displayed price in response to the AI pricing module. The system may also display replacement accepted, limit reached, abuse charge applied, price reduced, batch blocked, product quality warning, or operator review required. The display may be integrated with the vending machine chassis or mounted externally.
I. Sensor Placement and Calibration
[0049] In some embodiments, the disposal bin load cell is mounted under a floor of the disposal bin between a bin floor and a load-bearing frame. The load cell may include vibration-damping pads and factory calibration. The controller may perform auto-tare at the start of each transaction, at the start of each batch, or after a bin emptying event.
[0050] In some embodiments, the hopper load cell or single central load cell is positioned beneath the hopper, integrated into a load-bearing frame, or coupled to a floating hopper base. The hopper may be tared upon batch replenishment, after leveling, after cleaning, after a detected shock, after an operator command, or periodically during operation. Optional cameras may be positioned above the hopper to capture the customer access region and classify spoilage or product distribution.
J. Abuse Detection and Anomaly Handling
[0051] In some embodiments, the controller detects abuse based on exceeding the replacement threshold N. The controller may also detect anomalous disposal events based on unusually high item weight, repeated disposal events without purchase, disposal of non-product objects, disposal weight inconsistent with expected item weight, or camera-detected mismatch between the item removed from the hopper and the item placed into the disposal bin.
[0052] If abuse is detected, the system may add a charge to the current transaction, block further disposal, alert an operator, increase the customer-specific threshold risk score, require identity verification, lock the disposal bin lid, require remote review, or disable free replacement for the session. In some embodiments, the abuse rule charges the cost of all disposed items when n exceeds N, thereby discouraging intentional disposal of usable products.

The retrofit kit may include mechanical brackets, a hopper insert, a single central load cell, a disposal bin, an interface board, display software, and controller firmware.

The hopper, conical bottom, leveling actuator, disposal bin, single load cell, AI module, and display may be designed as an integrated module. The system may be deployed as a standalone vending machine, an autonomous kiosk, a cabinet within a 24/7 unattended store, or part of a networked fleet of machines.
L. Example Operation
[0055] In an example operation, an operator loads 10 kilograms of apples into the hopper and the controller records an initial hopper load weight. During customer transactions, customers may place spoiled apples into the disposal bin. Each disposal event is weighed and counted. The hopper inventory register remains unchanged by disposal events, while the disposal register increases. After multiple transactions, the AI pricing module calculates a spoilage index based on cumulative disposal weight divided by initial hopper load weight. If the spoilage index exceeds a threshold, the AI pricing module automatically reduces the displayed price and may notify the operator.
[0056] During the same operation, the automatic product-leveling system may periodically sweep or vibrate the product bed to prevent apples from bridging or accumulating at a side wall. The conical hopper bottom may direct apples toward the central weighing region, and the single central load cell may provide hopper weight data with reduced corner-load error. The controller may filter load-cell data during leveling and resume weighing after the product bed stabilizes.
